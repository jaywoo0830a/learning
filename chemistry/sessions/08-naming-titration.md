## Opening Attack

Try this:

> A student carefully measures **28.40 mL of 0.100 M NaOH (sodium hydroxide)** and uses it to neutralize **25.00 mL of an unknown $H_2SO_4$ (sulfuric acid)** solution.
>
> $$H_2SO_4 + 2NaOH \to Na_2SO_4 + 2H_2O$$
>
> (a) What is the molarity of the sulfuric acid?
>
> (b) Wait — $H_2SO_4$ has **two** acidic hydrogens. Does that matter? What if you forgot and treated it like HCl?
>
> (c) What is the name of the salt produced ($Na_2SO_4$)?

Stuck? Good. By the end of this session, you'll not only name every ionic compound, covalent compound, acid, and base you encounter — you'll also perform acid-base titrations with diprotic and triprotic acids like a pro.

## Worked Pattern

### Part A: Naming Ionic Compounds

> **Before we can talk about titration, we need to speak the language. What do we call the chemicals in the beaker?**

### Example 1 — Binary Ionic Compounds (Metal + Nonmetal)

**Problem:** Name these compounds and write their formulas.
(a) NaCl &emsp; (b) MgO &emsp; (c) $CaF_2$

---

**(a) NaCl — sodium chloride**

> **Pattern:** Name the metal first (sodium), then the nonmetal with **-ide** ending (chlorine → chloride).
>
> $\boxed{\text{Sodium chloride}}$

**(b) MgO — magnesium oxide**

> Metal: magnesium. Nonmetal: oxygen → oxide.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Magnesium oxide}}$

**(c) $CaF_2$ — calcium fluoride**

> Metal: calcium. Nonmetal: fluorine → fluoride.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Calcium fluoride}}$
>
> **Note:** The subscript 2 on F doesn't change the name. The name tells you what elements are present; the formula tells you how many.

---

**The pattern is: [Metal name] + [Nonmetal root + -ide]**

| Nonmetal | Root | Anion name |
|:--------:|:----:|:----------:|
| F (fluorine) | fluor- | fluoride |
| Cl (chlorine) | chlor- | chloride |
| Br (bromine) | brom- | bromide |
| I (iodine) | iod- | iodide |
| O (oxygen) | ox- | oxide |
| S (sulfur) | sulf- | sulfide |
| N (nitrogen) | nitr- | nitride |
| P (phosphorus) | phosph- | phosphide |

### Example 2 — Ionic Compounds with Transition Metals

**Problem:** Name these: (a) $FeCl_3$ &emsp; (b) $FeCl_2$ &emsp; (c) $CuO$ &emsp; (d) $Cu_2O$

---

**The challenge:** Iron can form $Fe^{2+}$ (iron(II)) or $Fe^{3+}$ (iron(III)). Copper can form $Cu^+$ (copper(I)) or $Cu^{2+}$ (copper(II)). The name must tell you WHICH charge.

**(a) $FeCl_3$ — iron(III) chloride**

> Cl has a $-1$ charge. Three Cl atoms = $-3$ total charge.
>
> $\therefore$ Fe must be $+3$ → **iron(III)**.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Iron(III) chloride}}$

**(b) $FeCl_2$ — iron(II) chloride**

> Two Cl atoms = $-2$ total charge.
>
> $\therefore$ Fe must be $+2$ → **iron(II)**.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Iron(II) chloride}}$

> **Key difference:** $FeCl_3$ and $FeCl_2$ are different compounds with different properties. The Roman numeral is NOT optional — it's the only way to distinguish them.

**(c) $CuO$ — copper(II) oxide**

> O is $-2$. One O = $-2$.
>
> $\therefore$ Cu must be $+2$ → copper(II).
>
> $\boxed{\text{Copper(II) oxide}}$

**(d) $Cu_2O$ — copper(I) oxide**

> O is $-2$. Two Cu must sum to $+2$.
>
> $\therefore$ Each Cu is $+1$ → copper(I).
>
> $\boxed{\text{Copper(I) oxide}}$

---

**Rule:** For transition metals with multiple possible charges, the Roman numeral = the charge on the metal ion.

**Common transition metal charges to memorize:**

| Metal | Possible charges | Examples |
|:-----:|:----------------:|----------|
| Fe (iron) | $+2, +3$ | $FeCl_2$ (II), $FeCl_3$ (III) |
| Cu (copper) | $+1, +2$ | $Cu_2O$ (I), $CuO$ (II) |
| Sn (tin) | $+2, +4$ | $SnCl_2$ (II), $SnCl_4$ (IV) |
| Pb (lead) | $+2, +4$ | $PbO$ (II), $PbO_2$ (IV) |
| Cr (chromium) | $+2, +3, +6$ | $CrCl_2$ (II), $CrCl_3$ (III) |
| Mn (manganese) | $+2, +4, +7$ | $MnCl_2$ (II), $MnO_2$ (IV) |

> **Metals with only ONE possible charge (no Roman numerals needed):** Group 1 ($Na^+, K^+$), Group 2 ($Mg^{2+}, Ca^{2+}$), $Ag^+, Zn^{2+}, Al^{3+}$.

### Example 3 — Polyatomic Ions

**Problem:** Name these: (a) $NaNO_3$ &emsp; (b) $CaCO_3$ &emsp; (c) $NH_4Cl$ &emsp; (d) $Mg_3(PO_4)_2$

---

**Polyatomic ions** are groups of atoms that stay together and carry a charge. You must memorize the most common ones.

**(a) $NaNO_3$ — sodium nitrate**

> $Na^+$ = sodium. $NO_3^-$ = nitrate.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Sodium nitrate}}$

**(b) $CaCO_3$ — calcium carbonate**

> $Ca^{2+}$ = calcium. $CO_3^{2-}$ = carbonate.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Calcium carbonate}}$

**(c) $NH_4Cl$ — ammonium chloride**

> $NH_4^+$ = ammonium. $Cl^-$ = chloride.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Ammonium chloride}}$

**(d) $Mg_3(PO_4)_2$ — magnesium phosphate**

> $Mg^{2+}$ = magnesium. $PO_4^{3-}$ = phosphate.
>
> Three $Mg^{2+}$ → $+6$ charge. Two $PO_4^{3-}$ → $-6$ charge. Neutral. ✓
>
> $\boxed{\text{Magnesium phosphate}}$

---

**The 10 polyatomic ions you MUST memorize:**

| Ion | Name | Ion | Name |
|:---:|:----:|:---:|:----:|
| $NH_4^+$ | ammonium | $NO_3^-$ | nitrate |
| $OH^-$ | hydroxide | $NO_2^-$ | nitrite |
| $CO_3^{2-}$ | carbonate | $SO_4^{2-}$ | sulfate |
| $HCO_3^-$ | hydrogen carbonate (bicarbonate) | $SO_3^{2-}$ | sulfite |
| $PO_4^{3-}$ | phosphate | $ClO_3^-$ | chlorate |

> **-ate vs. -ite:** The -ate ion has more oxygen atoms than the -ite ion. $NO_3^-$ (nitrate) has 3 O's, $NO_2^-$ (nitrite) has 2 O's. $SO_4^{2-}$ (sulfate) has 4 O's, $SO_3^{2-}$ (sulfite) has 3 O's.

---

### Example 4 — Writing Formulas from Names (Reverse Direction)

**Problem:** Write the formula for each compound from its name:
(a) Aluminum oxide &emsp; (b) Iron(III) sulfate &emsp; (c) Ammonium phosphate

---

**(a) Aluminum oxide**

> $Al^{3+}$ and $O^{2-}$.
>
> The charges must sum to zero. LCM of 3 and 2 = 6.
>
> $+3 \times 2 = +6$, $-2 \times 3 = -6$.
>
> $\therefore$ Two $Al^{3+}$ and three $O^{2-}$.
>
> $\boxed{Al_2O_3}$

**(b) Iron(III) sulfate**

> $Fe^{3+}$ (from the III) and $SO_4^{2-}$.
>
> LCM of 3 and 2 = 6.
>
> $+3 \times 2 = +6$, $-2 \times 3 = -6$.
>
> $\therefore$ Two $Fe^{3+}$ and three $SO_4^{2-}$.
>
> The sulfate ion goes in parentheses with a subscript 3.
>
> $\boxed{Fe_2(SO_4)_3}$

**(c) Ammonium phosphate**

> $NH_4^+$ and $PO_4^{3-}$.
>
> LCM of 1 and 3 = 3.
>
> $+1 \times 3 = +3$, $-3 \times 1 = -3$.
>
> $\therefore$ Three $NH_4^+$ and one $PO_4^{3-}$.
>
> $\boxed{(NH_4)_3PO_4}$

---

**The criss-cross method for formula writing:**

$$
\overset{+3}{Al} \; \overset{-2}{O} \;\to\; Al_2O_3
$$

The charge on Al (3+) becomes the subscript on O. The charge on O (2−) becomes the subscript on Al.

> **But always reduce to smallest whole numbers.** If you get $Ca_2O_2$, reduce to $CaO$.

### Example 5 — Naming Covalent Compounds (Nonmetal + Nonmetal)

**Problem:** Name these: (a) $CO$ &emsp; (b) $CO_2$ &emsp; (c) $N_2O_4$ &emsp; (d) $PCl_5$

---

**Covalent compounds** use Greek prefixes to indicate the number of each atom.

| Prefix | Number | Prefix | Number |
|:------:|:------:|:------:|:------:|
| mono- | 1 | hexa- | 6 |
| di- | 2 | hepta- | 7 |
| tri- | 3 | octa- | 8 |
| tetra- | 4 | nona- | 9 |
| penta- | 5 | deca- | 10 |

**(a) $CO$ — carbon monoxide**

> One C (no prefix needed for first element if only one). One O → mono- + oxide = monoxide.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Carbon monoxide}}$

**(b) $CO_2$ — carbon dioxide**

> One C. Two O → di- + oxide = dioxide.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Carbon dioxide}}$

**(c) $N_2O_4$ — dinitrogen tetroxide**

> Two N → di- + nitrogen = dinitrogen. Four O → tetra- + oxide = tetroxide.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Dinitrogen tetroxide}}$

**(d) $PCl_5$ — phosphorus pentachloride**

> One P (no prefix needed). Five Cl → penta- + chloride = pentachloride.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Phosphorus pentachloride}}$

> **Key rule:** Drop the "a" or "o" at the end of a prefix when the element name starts with a vowel. "monoxide" not "monooxide." "tetroxide" not "tetraoxide."

**Hydrates — the one naming rule you're missing:**

A **hydrate** is an ionic compound with water molecules trapped in its crystal. The dot means "with": $CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$ is read "copper(II) sulfate **with** five waters."

> **Name = [ionic compound name] + Greek prefix + "hydrate"** (no "water" or "aqua" in the name)

| Formula | Name |
|:-------:|:-----|
| $CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$ | copper(II) sulfate **pentahydrate** (penta- = 5) |
| $MgSO_4 \cdot 7H_2O$ | magnesium sulfate **heptahydrate** (hepta- = 7 — Epsom salt) |
| $Na_2CO_3 \cdot 10H_2O$ | sodium carbonate **decahydrate** (deca- = 10 — washing soda) |
| $BaCl_2 \cdot 2H_2O$ | barium chloride **dihydrate** (di- = 2) |
| $CaSO_4 \cdot 2H_2O$ | calcium sulfate **dihydrate** (gypsum) |

> **Two-step recipe:** (1) name the ionic compound exactly as in Examples 1–4 (with a Roman numeral if the metal has multiple charges); (2) count the waters and attach the Greek prefix + "hydrate." The water molecules are NOT part of the compound's name — they're attached to it.

### Example 6 — Naming Acids

**Problem:** Name these acids: (a) HCl &emsp; (b) $HNO_3$ &emsp; (c) $H_2SO_4$ &emsp; (d) $H_3PO_4$ &emsp; (e) $CH_3COOH$

---

**Acids naming rules:**

| Anion ending | Acid prefix/suffix | Example |
|:-----------:|:------------------:|:-------:|
| **-ide** → | **hydro-** + root + **-ic acid** | $Cl^-$ (chloride) → HCl (hydrochloric acid) |
| **-ate** → | root + **-ic acid** | $NO_3^-$ (nitrate) → $HNO_3$ (nitric acid) |
| **-ite** → | root + **-ous acid** | $NO_2^-$ (nitrite) → $HNO_2$ (nitrous acid) |

**(a) HCl — hydrochloric acid**

> $Cl^-$ is chloride (ends in -ide) → hydro-chlor-ic acid.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Hydrochloric acid}}$

**(b) $HNO_3$ — nitric acid**

> $NO_3^-$ is nitrate (ends in -ate) → nitr-ic acid.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Nitric acid}}$

**(c) $H_2SO_4$ — sulfuric acid**

> $SO_4^{2-}$ is sulfate (ends in -ate) → sulfur-ic acid.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Sulfuric acid}}$

**(d) $H_3PO_4$ — phosphoric acid**

> $PO_4^{3-}$ is phosphate (ends in -ate) → phosphor-ic acid.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Phosphoric acid}}$

**(e) $CH_3COOH$ — acetic acid**

> This is a weak organic acid. The acidic hydrogen is the one attached to O, not C.
>
> Often written as $HC_2H_3O_2$ in general chemistry.
>
> $\boxed{\text{Acetic acid}}$

---

**Common strong acids (memorize these):**

| Formula | Name | Key fact |
|:-------:|:----:|:--------:|
| HCl | hydrochloric acid | monoprotic |
| $HNO_3$ | nitric acid | monoprotic, strong oxidizer |
| $H_2SO_4$ | sulfuric acid | **diprotic** — two $H^+$ |
| $HClO_4$ | perchloric acid | monoprotic, very strong |
| $HBr$ | hydrobromic acid | monoprotic |
| $HI$ | hydroiodic acid | monoprotic |

**Common weak acids (memorize these):**

| Formula | Name | Key fact |
|:-------:|:----:|:--------:|
| $CH_3COOH$ | acetic acid | monoprotic, $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| $H_3PO_4$ | phosphoric acid | **triprotic** — three $H^+$ |
| $H_2CO_3$ | carbonic acid | diprotic, $CO_2$ in water |
| $HF$ | hydrofluoric acid | monoprotic, etches glass |

**The oxyacid family — where "hypo-" and "per-" come from (memorize this!):**

Chlorine forms a family of four oxyacids. The prefixes and suffixes tell you how many oxygen atoms each one has:

| # of O | Anion | Acid | # of O vs. -ate |
|:------:|:-----:|:----:|:---------------:|
| 1 | $ClO^-$ (hypochlorite) | $HClO$ (hypochlorous acid) | **hypo-** = one FEWER O than -ite |
| 2 | $ClO_2^-$ (chlorite) | $HClO_2$ (chlorous acid) | -ite → **-ous** |
| 3 | $ClO_3^-$ (chlorate) | $HClO_3$ (chloric acid) | -ate → **-ic** |
| 4 | $ClO_4^-$ (perchlorate) | $HClO_4$ (perchloric acid) | **per-** = one MORE O than -ate |

> **The pattern:** adding an O moves you up the ladder — hypochlorite (1 O) → chlorite (2 O) → chlorate (3 O) → perchlorate (4 O). The names track the oxygen count: **hypo-** (fewest), no prefix, **per-** (most).
>
> This is why the strong-acid table lists **perchloric acid** — it has the most oxygens. More oxygens spread out the negative charge on the conjugate base ($ClO_4^-$), which is why $HClO_4$ is one of the strongest acids known.

**(b) $Ca(OH)_2$ — calcium hydroxide**

>
> $\boxed{\text{Calcium hydroxide}}$

**(c) $NH_3$ — ammonia**

> $NH_3 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+ + OH^-$
>

### Part B: Acid-Base Titration

Now that you can name the chemicals, let's put them to use.

### Example 8 — Titration: Monoprotic Acid + Monoprotic Base

**Problem:** It takes 25.00 mL of 0.100 M NaOH to neutralize 20.00 mL of HCl. What is the molarity of the HCl?
$$HCl + NaOH \to NaCl + H_2O$$

---

**Step 1:** Moles of base (the known solution — the **titrant**).

> $\text{mol NaOH} = M \times V(\text{L}) = 0.100 \text{ M} \times 0.02500 \text{ L} = 0.00250 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Mole ratio from the balanced equation.

> $1 \text{ mol NaOH} : 1 \text{ mol HCl}$ (1:1 ratio for monoprotic).
>
> $\text{mol HCl} = 0.00250 \text{ mol}$

**Step 3:** Molarity of the acid.

> $M_{\text{HCl}} = \dfrac{\text{mol}}{V(\text{L})} = \dfrac{0.00250 \text{ mol}}{0.02000 \text{ L}} = 0.125 \text{ M}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{0.125 \text{ M HCl}}$.

---

### Example 9 — Titration: The Opening Problem (Diprotic Acid)

**Problem:** 28.40 mL of 0.100 M NaOH neutralizes 25.00 mL of $H_2SO_4$.
$$H_2SO_4 + 2NaOH \to Na_2SO_4 + 2H_2O$$
Find $M_{H_2SO_4}$.

---

**Step 1:** Moles of base.

> $\text{mol NaOH} = 0.100 \text{ M} \times 0.02840 \text{ L} = 0.002840 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Mole ratio. $H_2SO_4$ is **diprotic** — each molecule donates 2 $H^+$.

> $2 \text{ mol NaOH} : 1 \text{ mol } H_2SO_4$
>
> $\text{mol } H_2SO_4 = 0.002840 \text{ mol NaOH} \times \dfrac{1 \text{ mol } H_2SO_4}{2 \text{ mol NaOH}} = 0.001420 \text{ mol } H_2SO_4$

**Step 3:** Molarity.

> $M_{H_2SO_4} = \dfrac{0.001420 \text{ mol}}{0.02500 \text{ L}} = 0.05680 \text{ M}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{0.0568 \text{ M } H_2SO_4}$ (3 sig figs).

---

## ⚠️ THE TRAP — Forgetting the Diprotic Factor

**The wrong way:**

> A student forgets that $H_2SO_4$ is diprotic.
>
> *Wrong work:* "Moles NaOH = 0.100 × 0.02840 = 0.002840. Ratio is 1:1 (treating it like HCl). $M_{H_2SO_4} = 0.002840 / 0.02500 = \color{red}{0.1136 \text{ M}}$." ← **WRONG!**

**Why is this wrong?** Each $H_2SO_4$ molecule releases **2** $H^+$, so it takes **2** NaOH molecules to neutralize it. The 1:1 ratio treats $H_2SO_4$ as if it were monoprotic, giving an answer that's **exactly 2× too large**.

**The right way:**

> **Step 1:** Same moles of NaOH = 0.002840 mol.
>
> **Step 2:** $2 \text{ NaOH} : 1 \text{ } H_2SO_4$, NOT 1:1.
>
> $\text{mol } H_2SO_4 = 0.002840 \div 2 = 0.001420 \text{ mol}$
>
> **Step 3:** $M = 0.001420 / 0.02500 = 0.0568 \text{ M}$

**Spot the difference:**

| | Wrong (1:1) | Correct (2:1) |
|---|:-----------:|:-------------:|
| mol $H_2SO_4$ | 0.002840 | 0.001420 |
| Molarity | 0.114 M | **0.0568 M** |

The wrong answer is exactly **double** the correct answer. On an exam, both answers will be options — don't pick the wrong one.

### Example 10 — Titration: The Shortcut Formula

**Problem:** 35.00 mL of 0.200 M $H_3PO_4$ is titrated with 0.500 M NaOH. $H_3PO_4$ is **triprotic** (3 $H^+$). What volume of NaOH is needed to completely neutralize the acid?

---

**The shortcut:** At the equivalence point:

$$n_a M_a V_a = n_b M_b V_b$$

where $n_a$ = number of $H^+$ from the acid, $n_b$ = number of $OH^-$ from the base.

**Step 1:** Identify $n_a$ and $n_b$.

> $H_3PO_4$ has 3 acidic hydrogens → $n_a = 3$.
>
> NaOH has 1 $OH^-$ per formula → $n_b = 1$.

**Step 2:** Plug into $n_a M_a V_a = n_b M_b V_b$.

> $3 \times 0.200 \text{ M} \times 0.03500 \text{ L} = 1 \times 0.500 \text{ M} \times V_b$
>
> $0.02100 = 0.500 \times V_b$

**Step 3:** Solve for $V_b$.

> $V_b = \dfrac{0.02100}{0.500} = 0.04200 \text{ L} = 42.00 \text{ mL}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{42.0 \text{ mL of 0.500 M NaOH}}$ (3 sig figs).

### Example 11 — Titration: Finding the Unknown Acid's Identity

**Problem:** A 0.500 g sample of an unknown diprotic acid is dissolved in water and titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. It takes 45.00 mL to reach the endpoint. What is the molar mass of the acid? Can you identify it?

---

**Step 1:** Moles of base.

> $\text{mol NaOH} = 0.100 \times 0.04500 = 0.004500 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Moles of acid (diprotic → 2 $H^+$ per molecule).

> $\text{mol acid} = 0.004500 \text{ mol NaOH} \times \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.002250 \text{ mol}$

**Step 3:** Molar mass = grams ÷ moles.

> $M_{\text{acid}} = \dfrac{0.500 \text{ g}}{0.002250 \text{ mol}} = 222.2 \text{ g/mol}$

**Step 4:** Compare to known diprotic acids.

> Oxalic acid ($H_2C_2O_4$) = 90.03 g/mol — too low.
>
> Sulfuric acid ($H_2SO_4$) = 98.09 g/mol — too low.
>
> Malonic acid ($H_2C_3H_2O_4$) = 104.06 g/mol — too low.
>
> **No common diprotic acid matches 222 g/mol.** This could be a less common diprotic acid.

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{222 \text{ g/mol}}$ — the molar mass of the unknown diprotic acid.

> **Real-world application:** This is exactly how chemists determine the molar mass and identity of unknown acids found in natural products, food additives, or environmental samples.

### Example 12 — Titration: Back-Calculating Purity

**Problem:** A 2.000 g sample of impure $H_2C_2O_4 \cdot 2H_2O$ (oxalic acid dihydrate) is dissolved and titrated with 0.150 M NaOH. It takes 35.60 mL to reach the endpoint. What is the percent purity of the sample?
$$H_2C_2O_4 + 2NaOH \to Na_2C_2O_4 + 2H_2O$$

---

**Step 1:** Moles of base.

> $\text{mol NaOH} = 0.150 \times 0.03560 = 0.005340 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Moles of acid (diprotic).

> $\text{mol } H_2C_2O_4 = 0.005340 \div 2 = 0.002670 \text{ mol}$

**Step 3:** Mass of pure acid that this corresponds to.

> Molar mass $H_2C_2O_4 \cdot 2H_2O = 2(1.008) + 2(12.01) + 4(16.00) + 2(18.016) = 126.07 \text{ g/mol}$
>
> $\text{mass pure} = 0.002670 \text{ mol} \times 126.07 \text{ g/mol} = 0.3366 \text{ g}$

**Step 4:** Percent purity.

> $\% \text{ purity} = \dfrac{0.3366}{2.000} \times 100\% = 16.83\%$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{16.8\% \text{ purity}}$ — the sample is mostly filler, with only 16.8% oxalic acid dihydrate.

## What We Just Did — The Naming & Titration Procedures

### Naming Ionic Compounds — 3 Steps

**Step 1: Identify the cation (positive ion).**

> Group 1 or 2 metal? Use the element name (no Roman numeral).
>
> Transition metal? Determine the charge from the anion, then write the Roman numeral.
>
> Polyatomic cation? Use its name (e.g., ammonium $NH_4^+$).

**Step 2: Identify the anion (negative ion).**

> Monatomic (single element)? Root + **-ide** (chloride, oxide, etc.).
>
> Polyatomic? Use its memorized name (nitrate, sulfate, phosphate, etc.).

**Step 3: Write the cation name followed by the anion name.**

> If you wrote a formula from the name: criss-cross the charges (without signs) as subscripts, then reduce to smallest whole numbers.

---

### Titration Stoichiometry — 3 Steps

**Step 1: Calculate moles of the titrant (the solution of known concentration).**

> $\text{mol} = M \times V(\text{L})$. Remember: mL → L = divide by 1000.

**Step 2: Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation.**

> For monoprotic acids: 1:1 ratio with a monoprotic base.
>
> For diprotic acids ($H_2SO_4$): 2:1 ratio with NaOH.
>
> For triprotic acids ($H_3PO_4$): 3:1 ratio with NaOH.
>
> **Always check how many $H^+$ the acid donates.**

**Step 3: Convert moles of the unknown to the requested quantity.**

> Molarity? $M = \text{mol} / V(\text{L})$.
>
> Molar mass? $\text{MM} = \text{mass(g)} / \text{mol}$.
>
> Percent purity? $(\text{mass pure} / \text{mass impure}) \times 100\%$.
>
> Shortcut: $n_a M_a V_a = n_b M_b V_b$ works when the reaction ratio is $n_a : n_b$.

---

Copy these. Naming and titration are among the most practical skills in chemistry — you'll use them in every lab from now on.

## 💡 Pro Tips — Calculator Shortcuts & Mental Checks

**Tip 1 — The criss-cross never fails.** To write a formula from a name: take the charge of the cation (without the +), make it the subscript of the anion. Take the charge of the anion (without the −), make it the subscript of the cation. Then reduce. $Al^{3+}$ $O^{2-}$ → $Al_2O_3$. Works every time.

**Tip 2 — Polyatomic ions in parentheses.** When a polyatomic ion gets a subscript > 1, put it in parentheses: $(NO_3)_2$, $(SO_4)_3$, $(PO_4)_2$. Never write $CaNO_3NO_3$ — it's $Ca(NO_3)_2$.

**Tip 3 — The $n_a M_a V_a = n_b M_b V_b$ shortcut.** This is the fastest way to solve titration problems. For $H_2SO_4$ + NaOH: $n_a = 2$, $n_b = 1$. For $H_3PO_4$ + NaOH: $n_a = 3$, $n_b = 1$. For $H_2SO_4$ + $Ca(OH)_2$: $n_a = 2$, $n_b = 2$.

**Tip 4 — Diprotic ≠ twice as strong.** Don't confuse "diprotic" (donates 2 $H^+$) with "strong." $H_2SO_4$ is both diprotic and strong for its first $H^+$, but $H_2CO_3$ is diprotic and weak. The number of $H^+$ and the acid strength are independent properties.

**Tip 5 — Titration sanity check.** If your unknown acid molarity comes out higher than your base molarity (for a monoprotic acid), check your math — you probably forgot to convert mL to L. Typical titrations use similar concentrations (0.05–0.5 M).

## 📝 Key Terms — Quick Reference

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **ionic compound** | metal + nonmetal; held together by electrostatic attraction |
| **covalent compound** | nonmetal + nonmetal; shared electrons |
| **monatomic ion** | single atom with a charge ($Na^+, Cl^-$) |
| **polyatomic ion** | group of atoms with a charge ($NO_3^-, SO_4^{2-}$) |
| **-ide ending** | for monatomic anions: chloride, oxide, sulfide |
| **-ate / -ite** | -ate has more oxygen than -ite (nitrate $NO_3^-$ vs. nitrite $NO_2^-$) |
| **Roman numeral** | indicates the charge on a transition metal cation |
| **criss-cross method** | swap charges (without signs) as subscripts to write formulas |
| **Greek prefix** | mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, etc. — used in covalent compound names |
| **acid naming** | -ide → hydro- + -ic; -ate → -ic; -ite → -ous |
| **titration** | controlled addition of a known solution to find an unknown concentration |
| **titrant** | the solution of known concentration (in the burette) |
| **equivalence point** | exactly enough titrant added to react completely with the analyte |
| **monoprotic** | donates 1 $H^+$ (HCl, $HNO_3$, $CH_3COOH$) |
| **diprotic** | donates 2 $H^+$ ($H_2SO_4$, $H_2CO_3$) |
| **triprotic** | donates 3 $H^+$ ($H_3PO_4$) |
| **$n_a M_a V_a = n_b M_b V_b$** | shortcut formula for titration calculations |
| **percent purity** | (mass of pure substance / mass of sample) × 100% |
| **indicator** | substance that changes color at the endpoint (phenolphthalein, methyl orange) |
| **standard solution** | a solution of precisely known concentration (the titrant) |
| **hydrate** | ionic compound with trapped water; name = compound name + prefix + "hydrate" ($CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$ = pentahydrate) |
| **hypo- / per-** | hypo- = one fewer O than -ite; per- = one more O than -ate ($HClO$ vs. $HClO_4$) |
| **oxyacid ladder** | hypochlorous → chlorous → chloric → perchloric as oxygen count rises |
