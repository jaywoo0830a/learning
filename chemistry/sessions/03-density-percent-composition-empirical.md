## Worked Pattern

### Example 1 — Density from Mass and Volume

**Problem:** A metal cube has edge 2.00 cm and mass 42.0 g. Calculate its density.

**Step 1:** Calculate volume.

> Volume of cube = $(\text{edge})^3 = (2.00 \text{ cm})^3 = 8.00 \text{ cm}^3$

**Step 2:** Divide mass by volume.

> $\rho = \dfrac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} = \dfrac{42.0 \text{ g}}{8.00 \text{ cm}^3}$

**Step 3:** Calculate and apply sig figs.

> $42.0 \div 8.00 = 5.25 \text{ g/cm}^3$
>
> Sig figs: 42.0 (3) and 8.00 (3) → 3 sig figs → 5.25 g/cm³

**Answer:** $\boxed{5.25 \text{ g/cm}^3}$.

Compare: aluminum is 2.70, titanium is 4.51. This metal is denser than both — it's neither! (It could be germanium at 5.32 g/cm³.)

---

### Example 2 — Identifying an Unknown by Density

**Problem:** An irregular solid displaces water from 25.0 mL to 28.5 mL. Its mass is 28.7 g. Is it quartz ($\rho = 2.65$ g/cm³) or fluorite ($\rho = 3.18$ g/cm³)?

**Step 1:** Volume by displacement.

> $V = 28.5 \text{ mL} - 25.0 \text{ mL} = 3.5 \text{ mL}$
>
> Remember: $1 \text{ mL} = 1 \text{ cm}^3$, so $V = 3.5 \text{ cm}^3$.

**Step 2:** Calculate density.

> $\rho = 28.7 \text{ g} \div 3.5 \text{ cm}^3 = 8.2 \text{ g/cm}^3$

**Step 3:** Compare to known densities.

> 2.65 g/cm³ (quartz) — too low. 3.18 g/cm³ (fluorite) — also too low.
>
> 8.2 matches neither. (8.2 has 2 sig figs, from 3.5.)

**Answer:** $\boxed{8.2 \text{ g/cm}^3 \text{ — neither quartz nor fluorite}}$. The calculation is right — the unknown is something else.

---

### Example 3 — Mass Percent from a Formula

**Problem:** Calculate the mass percent of each element in $H_2O$ (water).

**Step 1:** Find molar mass of the compound.

> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{H: } &2 \times 1.008 = 2.016 \text{ g/mol} \\
> \text{O: } &1 \times 16.00 = 16.00 \text{ g/mol} \\ \hline
> \text{Total: } &2.016 + 16.00 = 18.016 \text{ g/mol}
> \end{aligned}
> $$

**Step 2:** Mass percent of each element = (mass of element in 1 mol / molar mass) × 100%.

> $\%\text{H} = \dfrac{2.016}{18.016} \times 100\% = 11.19\%$
>
> $\%\text{O} = \dfrac{16.00}{18.016} \times 100\% = 88.81\%$

**Step 3:** Verify: $11.19\% + 88.81\% = 100.00\%$. ✓

**Answer:** $\boxed{11.19\% \text{ H, } 88.81\% \text{ O by mass}}$.

---

### Example 4 — Mass Percent: Glucose

**Problem:** Calculate the mass percent of each element in $C_6H_{12}O_6$ (glucose).

**Step 1:** Molar mass.

> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{C: } &6 \times 12.01 = 72.06 \text{ g/mol} \\
> \text{H: } &12 \times 1.008 = 12.096 \text{ g/mol} \\
> \text{O: } &6 \times 16.00 = 96.00 \text{ g/mol} \\ \hline
> \text{Total: } &72.06 + 12.096 + 96.00 = 180.156 \text{ g/mol}
> \end{aligned}
> $$

**Step 2:** Percent of each.

> $\%\text{C} = (72.06 / 180.156) \times 100\% = 40.00\%$
>
> $\%\text{H} = (12.096 / 180.156) \times 100\% = 6.714\%$
>
> $\%\text{O} = (96.00 / 180.156) \times 100\% = 53.29\%$

**Step 3:** Sum check: $40.00 + 6.714 + 53.29 = 100.00\%$. ✓

**Answer:** $\boxed{40.00\% \text{ C, } 6.71\% \text{ H, } 53.3\% \text{ O by mass}}$ (4 sig figs throughout).

> Notice: This is very close to the opening problem's 40.0% C, 6.71% H, 53.3% O. Interesting...

### Example 5 — Empirical Formula from Mass Percent

**Problem:** A compound is 40.0% C, 6.71% H, 53.3% O by mass. Find the empirical formula.

**Step 1:** Assume exactly 100.0 g of the compound.

> C: 40.0 g &emsp; H: 6.71 g &emsp; O: 53.3 g

**Step 2:** Convert each mass to moles by dividing by molar mass.

> $\text{mol C} = 40.0 \text{ g} \div 12.01 \text{ g/mol} = 3.3305... \text{ mol}$
>
> $\text{mol H} = 6.71 \text{ g} \div 1.008 \text{ g/mol} = 6.6567... \text{ mol}$
>
> $\text{mol O} = 53.3 \text{ g} \div 16.00 \text{ g/mol} = 3.33125... \text{ mol}$

**Step 3:** Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value.

> Smallest: ~3.33 (both C and O are close)
>
> C: $3.3305 / 3.3305 = 1.00$
>
> H: $6.6567 / 3.3305 = 2.00$
>
> O: $3.3313 / 3.3305 = 1.00$
>
> Ratio: C : H : O = 1 : 2 : 1

**Answer:** $\boxed{CH_2O}$ (empirical formula).

This is the simplest whole-number ratio. The molecular formula could be $CH_2O$, $C_2H_4O_2$, $C_3H_6O_3$, etc.

---

### Example 6 — Empirical Formula: When the Ratio Isn't Integer

**Problem:** A compound is 69.6% Ba, 6.09% C, 24.3% O. Find the empirical formula.

**Step 1:** Assume 100.0 g.

> $\text{mol Ba} = 69.6 \div 137.33 = 0.5068 \text{ mol}$
>
> $\text{mol C} = 6.09 \div 12.01 = 0.5071 \text{ mol}$
>
> $\text{mol O} = 24.3 \div 16.00 = 1.5188 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Divide by smallest (0.5068).

> Ba: $0.5068 / 0.5068 = 1.00$
>
> C: $0.5071 / 0.5068 = 1.00$
>
> O: $1.5188 / 0.5068 = 3.00$

**Step 3:** All are integers. Ratio = 1 : 1 : 3.

**Answer:** $\boxed{BaCO_3}$ (barium carbonate).

---

### Example 7 — Empirical Formula: When You Get a .5 (Multiply by 2)

**Problem:** A compound is 30.4% N and 69.6% O. Find the empirical formula.

**Step 1:** Assume 100.0 g.

> $\text{mol N} = 30.4 \div 14.01 = 2.170 \text{ mol}$
>
> $\text{mol O} = 69.6 \div 16.00 = 4.350 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Divide by smallest (2.170).

> N: $2.170 / 2.170 = 1.00$
>
> O: $4.350 / 2.170 = 2.00$

**Step 3:** Integers! Ratio = 1 : 2.

**Answer:** $\boxed{NO_2}$ (nitrogen dioxide).

---

### Example 8 — Molecular Formula from Empirical Formula + Molar Mass

**Problem:** A compound has empirical formula $CH_2O$ and molar mass 180.2 g/mol. What is the molecular formula?

**Step 1:** Calculate the empirical formula mass.

> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{C: } &12.01 \\
> \text{H: } &2 \times 1.008 = 2.016 \\
> \text{O: } &16.00 \\ \hline
> CH_2O \text{ mass: } &12.01 + 2.016 + 16.00 = 30.026 \text{ g/mol}
> \end{aligned}
> $$

**Step 2:** Divide molecular mass by empirical mass.

> $n = 180.2 \div 30.026 = 6.00$

**Step 3:** Multiply all subscripts in the empirical formula by $n$.

> $C_1H_2O_1 \times 6 = C_6H_{12}O_6$

**Answer:** $\boxed{C_6H_{12}O_6}$ (glucose).

---

## ⚠️ THE TRAP — Forgetting to Divide by the Smallest

**The wrong way:**

> A compound is 75.0% C and 25.0% H.
>
> *Wrong work:* Assume 100 g. C: 75.0/12.01 = 6.24 mol. H: 25.0/1.008 = 24.8 mol.
> "So the formula is $\color{red}{C_{6.24}H_{24.8}}$" ← **WRONG!**

**Why is this wrong?** Chemical formulas use whole numbers. You must divide all mole values by the smallest before you can read the ratio.

**The right way:**

> **Step 1:** C: 75.0 g → 75.0 / 12.01 = 6.245 mol. H: 25.0 g → 25.0 / 1.008 = 24.80 mol.
>
> **Step 2:** Divide by smallest (6.245):
>
> C: $6.245 / 6.245 = 1.00$
>
> H: $24.80 / 6.245 = 3.97 \approx 4$
>
> **Formula: $CH_4$** (methane)

**Spot the difference:** Raw mole values mean nothing. The ratio AFTER dividing by the smallest gives the subscripts.

---

## What We Just Did — The Empirical Formula Procedure

### Step 1: Convert mass percent to grams by assuming exactly 100.0 g of compound.

> "40.0% C" means 40.0 g of C in every 100.0 g of compound.

### Step 2: Convert each mass to moles by dividing by the element's molar mass.

> $\text{mol} = \text{grams} \div \text{(g/mol from the periodic table)}$
>
> Keep at least 4 decimal places — do NOT round yet.

### Step 3: Divide every mole value by the smallest mole value.

> You now have a ratio of small whole numbers.
>
> If you get **.5**, multiply ALL numbers by 2.
>
> If you get **.33** or **.67**, multiply ALL numbers by 3.
>
> These whole numbers become the subscripts in the empirical formula.

---

Copy this. This 3-step procedure works for every empirical formula problem you will ever see.

---

## 💡 Pro Tips — Calculator Shortcuts & Mental Checks

**Tip 1 — The "divide by smallest" step is non-negotiable.** Raw mole values are never the final subscripts. Always divide. If you skip this step, your formula will be wrong 100% of the time.

**Tip 2 — Handle .5, .33, .67 with multiplication, not rounding.** If after dividing you get 1 : 1.5, multiply BOTH by 2 to get 2 : 3 — not 1 : 2. If you get 1 : 1.33, multiply by 3 to get 3 : 4. Never round 1.5 to 2 or 1.33 to 1.

**Tip 3 — Percent sum must equal 100% (or very close).** If the given percents add to 97% or 103%, there's either an unlisted element, rounding, or experimental error. On exams, they should sum to 100.00%.

**Tip 4 — Empirical ≠ molecular.** $CH_2O$ could be formaldehyde ($CH_2O$), acetic acid ($C_2H_4O_2$), lactic acid ($C_3H_6O_3$), or glucose ($C_6H_{12}O_6$). You need the molar mass to tell which one it is.

**Tip 5 — Density identification trick.** Calculate density → compare to list → if it doesn't match ANY listed value, don't force a match. Say "none of the above." Many students lose points by forcing a near-match (e.g., calling 5.25 "close enough" to 4.51). Density is precise — a mismatch is a mismatch.

## �📝 Key Terms — Quick Reference

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **density** | $\rho = m/V$; characteristic property used to identify substances |
| **water displacement** | $V_{\text{object}} = V_{\text{final}} - V_{\text{initial}}$; used for irregular solids |
| **percent composition** | (mass of element in 1 mol ÷ molar mass) × 100% |
| **empirical formula** | simplest whole-number atom ratio (e.g., $CH_2O$, not $C_6H_{12}O_6$) |
| **molecular formula** | actual number of atoms per molecule ($n \times$ empirical formula) |
| **$n = M_{\text{molecular}} / M_{\text{empirical}}$** | the multiplier to go from empirical → molecular |
| **hydrate** | ionic compound with water trapped in crystal ($MgSO_4 \cdot 7H_2O$) |
| **anhydrous** | the salt after water is driven off by heating |
| **divide by smallest** | the critical step in empirical formulas — raw mole values are NOT subscripts |
| **.5 → ×2, .33/.67 → ×3** | when the ratio isn't integer, multiply ALL numbers by 2 or 3 |
