## Worked Pattern

### Example 1 — Density from Mass and Volume

**Problem:** A metal cube has edge 2.00 cm and mass 42.0 g. Calculate its density.

```
Step 1: Calculate volume.
  Volume of cube = (edge)³ = (2.00 cm)³ = 8.00 cm³

Step 2: Divide mass by volume.
  Density = mass / volume = 42.0 g / 8.00 cm³

Step 3: Calculate and apply sig figs.
  42.0 / 8.00 = 5.25 g/cm³
  Sig figs: 42.0 (3) and 8.00 (3) → 3 sig figs → 5.25 g/cm³
```

**Density = 5.25 g/cm³.** Compare: aluminum is 2.70, titanium is 4.51. This metal is denser than both — it's neither! (It could be germanium at 5.32 g/cm³, or perhaps a measurement error — but the calculation is correct.)

### Example 2 — Identifying an Unknown by Density

**Problem:** An irregular solid displaces water from 25.0 mL to 28.5 mL. Its mass is 28.7 g. Is it quartz ($\rho$ = 2.65 g/cm³) or fluorite ($\rho$ = 3.18 g/cm³)?

```
Step 1: Volume by displacement.
  V = 28.5 mL − 25.0 mL = 3.5 mL
  Remember: 1 mL = 1 cm³, so V = 3.5 cm³.

Step 2: Calculate density.
  ρ = 28.7 g / 3.5 cm³ = 8.2 g/cm³

Step 3: Compare to known densities.
  2.65 g/cm³ (quartz) — too low.
  3.18 g/cm³ (fluorite) — also too low.
  8.2 matches neither. Something's off — recheck the data.
  But the calculation itself is correct. (8.2 has 2 sig figs, from 3.5.)
```

**Density = 8.2 g/cm³.** Neither. The calculation is right — the unknown is something else.

### Example 3 — Mass Percent from a Formula

**Problem:** Calculate the mass percent of each element in $H_2O$.

```
Step 1: Find molar mass of the compound.
  H: 2 × 1.008 = 2.016 g/mol
  O: 1 × 16.00 = 16.00 g/mol
  H₂O total = 2.016 + 16.00 = 18.016 g/mol

Step 2: Mass percent of each element = (mass of element in 1 mol / molar mass) × 100%.

  %H = (2.016 / 18.016) × 100% = 11.19%
  %O = (16.00 / 18.016) × 100% = 88.81%

Step 3: Verify: 11.19% + 88.81% = 100.00%. ✓
```

**Answer: 11.19% H, 88.81% O by mass.**

---

### Example 4 — Mass Percent: Another One

**Problem:** Calculate the mass percent of each element in $C_6H_{12}O_6$ (glucose).

```
Step 1: Molar mass.
  C: 6 × 12.01 = 72.06 g/mol
  H: 12 × 1.008 = 12.096 g/mol
  O: 6 × 16.00 = 96.00 g/mol
  Total = 72.06 + 12.096 + 96.00 = 180.156 g/mol

Step 2: Percent of each.
  %C = (72.06 / 180.156) × 100% = 40.00%
  %H = (12.096 / 180.156) × 100% = 6.714%
  %O = (96.00 / 180.156) × 100% = 53.29%

Step 3: Sum check: 40.00 + 6.714 + 53.29 = 100.00%. ✓
```

**Answer: 40.00% C, 6.71% H, 53.3% O by mass** (4 sig figs throughout).

Notice: This is very close to the opening problem's 40.0% C, 6.71% H, 53.3% O. Interesting...

### Example 5 — Empirical Formula from Mass Percent

**Problem:** A compound is 40.0% C, 6.71% H, 53.3% O by mass. Find the empirical formula.

```
Step 1: Assume exactly 100.0 g of the compound.
  C: 40.0 g
  H: 6.71 g
  O: 53.3 g

Step 2: Convert each mass to moles by dividing by molar mass.
  mol C = 40.0 g ÷ 12.01 g/mol = 3.3305... mol
  mol H = 6.71 g ÷ 1.008 g/mol = 6.6567... mol
  mol O = 53.3 g ÷ 16.00 g/mol = 3.33125... mol

Step 3: Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value.
  Smallest: ~3.33 (both C and O are close)

  C: 3.3305 / 3.3305 = 1.00
  H: 6.6567 / 3.3305 = 2.00
  O: 3.3313 / 3.3305 = 1.00

  Ratio: C : H : O = 1 : 2 : 1
```

**Empirical formula: $CH_2O$.**

This is the simplest whole-number ratio. The molecular formula could be $CH_2O$, $C_2H_4O_2$, $C_3H_6O_3$, etc.

### Example 6 — Empirical Formula: When the Ratio Isn't Integer

**Problem:** A compound is 69.6% Ba, 6.09% C, 24.3% O. Find the empirical formula.

```
Step 1: Assume 100.0 g.
  Ba: 69.6 g → 69.6 / 137.33 = 0.5068 mol
  C:   6.09 g → 6.09 / 12.01 = 0.5071 mol
  O:  24.3 g → 24.3 / 16.00 = 1.5188 mol

Step 2: Divide by smallest (0.5068).
  Ba: 0.5068 / 0.5068 = 1.00
  C:  0.5071 / 0.5068 = 1.00
  O:  1.5188 / 0.5068 = 3.00

Step 3: All are integers. Ratio = 1 : 1 : 3.
```

**Empirical formula: $BaCO_3$** (barium carbonate).

---

### Example 7 — Empirical Formula: When You Get a .5

**Problem:** A compound is 30.4% N and 69.6% O. Find the empirical formula.

```
Step 1: Assume 100.0 g.
  N: 30.4 g → 30.4 / 14.01 = 2.170 mol
  O: 69.6 g → 69.6 / 16.00 = 4.350 mol

Step 2: Divide by smallest (2.170).
  N: 2.170 / 2.170 = 1.00
  O: 4.350 / 2.170 = 2.00

Step 3: Integers! Ratio = 1 : 2.
```

**Empirical formula: $NO_2$.**

---

### Example 8 — Molecular Formula from Empirical Formula + Molar Mass

**Problem:** A compound has empirical formula $CH_2O$ and molar mass 180.2 g/mol. What is the molecular formula?

```
Step 1: Calculate the empirical formula mass.
  C: 12.01
  H: 2 × 1.008 = 2.016
  O: 16.00
  CH₂O mass = 12.01 + 2.016 + 16.00 = 30.026 g/mol

Step 2: Divide molecular mass by empirical mass.
  n = 180.2 / 30.026 = 6.00

Step 3: Multiply all subscripts in the empirical formula by n.
  C₁H₂O₁ × 6 = C₆H₁₂O₆
```

**Molecular formula: $C_6H_{12}O_6$** (glucose).

---

## ⚠️ THE TRAP — Forgetting to Divide by the Smallest

**The wrong way:**

> A compound is 75.0% C and 25.0% H.
>
> *Wrong work:* Assume 100 g. C: 75.0/12.01 = 6.24 mol. H: 25.0/1.008 = 24.8 mol.
> "So the formula is C₆.₂₄H₂₄.₈" ← WRONG!

**Why is this wrong?** Chemical formulas use whole numbers. You must divide all mole values by the smallest before you can read the ratio.

**The right way:**

```
C: 75.0 g → 75.0 / 12.01 = 6.245 mol
H: 25.0 g → 25.0 / 1.008 = 24.80 mol

Divide by smallest (6.245):
  C: 6.245 / 6.245 = 1.00
  H: 24.80 / 6.245 = 3.97 ≈ 4

Formula: CH₄ (methane)
```

**Spot the difference:** Raw mole values mean nothing. The ratio AFTER dividing by the smallest gives the subscripts.

## What We Just Did — The Empirical Formula Procedure

```
WHAT WE JUST DID — The Empirical Formula Procedure

Step 1: Convert mass percent to grams by assuming exactly 100.0 g of compound.
  → "40.0% C" means 40.0 g of C in every 100.0 g of compound.

Step 2: Convert each mass to moles by dividing by the element's molar mass.
  → mol = grams ÷ (g/mol from the periodic table)
  → Keep at least 4 decimal places — do NOT round yet.

Step 3: Divide every mole value by the smallest mole value.
  → You now have a ratio of small whole numbers.
  → If you get .5, multiply ALL numbers by 2. If .33 or .67, multiply by 3.
  → These whole numbers become the subscripts in the empirical formula.
```

Copy this. This 3-step procedure works for every empirical formula problem you will ever see.

## Advanced Drills (A1–A10)

> Goal: Multi-step integration with Sessions 01–02. Exam traps.

**A1.** (Multi-step with Session 02) A cylinder of unknown metal has radius 1.50 cm, height 5.00 cm, and mass 250.0 g. Volume = $\pi r^2 h$. (a) Calculate volume in cm³. (b) Calculate density. (c) Use the density to identify the metal from this list: aluminum (2.70), iron (7.87), copper (8.96), lead (11.3 g/cm³).

**A2.** (Multi-step with Session 01) Three trials measure the mass and volume of an unknown liquid:

| Trial | Mass (g) | Volume (mL) |
|:-----:|:--------:|:-----------:|
| 1 | 14.20 | 18.0 |
| 2 | 15.05 | 19.0 |
| 3 | 13.88 | 17.5 |

Calculate density for each trial with correct sig figs. Then calculate the average density. Is this liquid water (1.00 g/mL), ethanol (0.789 g/mL), or something else?

**A3.** (Trap-laden) A compound is found to be 30.4% nitrogen and 69.6% oxygen. A student writes: "N: 30.4/14.01 = 2.17. O: 69.6/16.00 = 4.35. Ratio is 2.17:4.35. Multiply by 2 → 4.34:8.70. Still not integers." What did the student do wrong? Solve correctly.

**A4.** (Reverse engineering) A student calculates the empirical formula of a compound from: C = 40.0%, H = 6.7%, O = 53.3%. Their answer is $C_3H_6O_3$. Their partner says the empirical formula is $CH_2O$. Who is right? Explain the mistake.

**A5.** (Constructive) Write an exam problem where a compound's empirical formula and molecular formula are the SAME. (Hint: the molar mass must equal the empirical formula mass.) Solve it.

**A6.** (Constructive) Write a problem where the "divide by smallest" step gives a ratio ending in .33 or .67 (requiring multiplication by 3). Solve it and show why multiplication by 3 is needed.

Known densities: magnesium (1.74), aluminum (2.70), zinc (7.13), copper (8.96), lead (11.3), gold (19.3 g/cm³). Identify each metal. Show all calculations with correct sig figs.

**A8.** (Timed — under 3 min) A compound contains only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Combustion of 1.000 g of the compound produces 1.466 g of $CO_2$ and 0.600 g of $H_2O$. (a) Find the mass of C in the original sample (all C ends up in $CO_2$). (b) Find the mass of H (all H ends up in $H_2O$). (c) Find the mass of O by difference. (d) Determine the empirical formula.

**A9.** (Hardest variation) A hydrated salt has the formula $MgSO_4 \cdot xH_2O$. When 5.00 g of the hydrate is heated, 2.44 g of water is driven off. **A10.** (Exam boss) A 2.500 g sample of a compound containing only C, H, and O is burned in excess oxygen, producing 5.713 g $CO_2$ and 2.339 g $H_2O$. Separately, the molar mass is found to be 116.2 g/mol. (a) Determine the empirical formula. (b) Determine the molecular formula. (c) Calculate the mass percent of each element from the molecular formula and verify it matches the combustion data. Use correct sig figs throughout.
