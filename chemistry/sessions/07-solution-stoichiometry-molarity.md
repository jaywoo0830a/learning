## Opening Attack

Try this:

> You add **35.0 mL of 0.250 M $Pb(NO_3)_2$ (lead(II) nitrate)** to **50.0 mL of 0.180 M $NaCl$ (sodium chloride)**.
>
> A white precipitate of $PbCl_2$ (lead(II) chloride) forms:
> $$Pb(NO_3)_2 + 2NaCl \to PbCl_2\downarrow + 2NaNO_3$$
>
> (a) How many moles of $Pb^{2+}$ are in the first solution?
>
> (b) How many moles of $Cl^-$ are in the second solution?
>
> (c) Which is the limiting reagent?
>
> (d) How many **grams** of $PbCl_2$ precipitate?

Stuck? Good. By the end of this session, you'll convert solution volumes and concentrations to moles, handle dilution, and solve precipitation stoichiometry — all in your head.

## Worked Pattern

### Example 1 — Molarity from Mass and Volume

**Problem:** You dissolve 5.84 g of NaCl (sodium chloride) in enough water to make 250.0 mL of solution. What is the molarity?

---

**Step 1:** Convert mass to moles.

> Molar mass NaCl = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol
>
> $\text{mol NaCl} = 5.84 \text{ g} \div 58.44 \text{ g/mol} = 0.09993 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Convert volume to liters.

> $250.0 \text{ mL} = 0.2500 \text{ L}$ (This is the #1 place students lose points — forgetting this conversion!)

**Step 3:** Divide moles by liters.

> $M = \dfrac{\text{mol}}{\text{L}} = \dfrac{0.09993 \text{ mol}}{0.2500 \text{ L}} = 0.3997 \text{ M}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{0.400 \text{ M NaCl}}$ (3 sig figs, limited by 5.84).

$$
M = \frac{\text{mol}}{\text{L}} \qquad \text{mol} = M \times V(\text{L}) \qquad V(\text{L}) = \frac{\text{mol}}{M}
$$

---

### Example 2 — Moles from Molarity and Volume

**Problem:** How many moles of $H_2SO_4$ (sulfuric acid) are in 35.0 mL of 0.250 M $H_2SO_4$?

---

**Step 1:** Convert mL to L.

> $35.0 \text{ mL} = 0.0350 \text{ L}$

**Step 2:** Multiply molarity by liters.

> $\text{mol } H_2SO_4 = M \times V = 0.250 \text{ mol/L} \times 0.0350 \text{ L}$
>
> $\phantom{\text{mol } H_2SO_4} = 0.00875 \text{ mol}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{8.75 \times 10^{-3} \text{ mol } H_2SO_4}$.

---

### Example 3 — Dilution: $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$

**Problem:** You have 50.0 mL of 2.00 M HCl. You add water until the total volume is 200.0 mL. What is the new concentration?

---

**Step 1:** Identify knowns.

> $M_1 = 2.00 \text{ M}$, $V_1 = 50.0 \text{ mL}$, $V_2 = 200.0 \text{ mL}$

**Step 2:** Apply $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$.

> $M_2 = \dfrac{M_1 V_1}{V_2} = \dfrac{2.00 \text{ M} \times 50.0 \text{ mL}}{200.0 \text{ mL}}$

**Step 3:** Solve.

> $M_2 = \dfrac{100.0}{200.0} = 0.500 \text{ M}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{0.500 \text{ M HCl}}$.

> **Key insight:** $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ works because **moles don't change during dilution** — you're just spreading the same number of moles through more water. The units of $V$ can be mL (they cancel) as long as both are the same unit.

### Example 4 — Solution Stoichiometry (Simple)

**Problem:** How many grams of $AgCl$ (silver chloride) precipitate when 25.0 mL of 0.100 M $AgNO_3$ (silver nitrate) reacts with excess NaCl?
$$AgNO_3 + NaCl \to AgCl\downarrow + NaNO_3$$

---

**Step 1:** mol $AgNO_3$ from $M$ and $V$.

> $\text{mol } AgNO_3 = 0.100 \text{ M} \times 0.0250 \text{ L} = 0.00250 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Mole ratio to product.

> From equation: $1 \text{ mol } AgNO_3 \to 1 \text{ mol } AgCl$
>
> $\text{mol } AgCl = 0.00250 \text{ mol}$

**Step 3:** Moles to grams.

> Molar mass $AgCl = 107.87 + 35.45 = 143.32 \text{ g/mol}$
>
> $\text{g } AgCl = 0.00250 \text{ mol} \times 143.32 \text{ g/mol} = 0.3583 \text{ g}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{0.358 \text{ g } AgCl}$.

$$
\text{mL} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,1000}\; \text{L} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,M}\; \text{mol} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,\text{mole ratio}}\; \text{mol product} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,\text{MM}}\; \text{g product}
$$

### Example 5 — Precipitation with Limiting Reagent

**Problem:** 35.0 mL of 0.250 M $Pb(NO_3)_2$ + 50.0 mL of 0.180 M NaCl. How many grams of $PbCl_2$ precipitate?
$$Pb(NO_3)_2 + 2NaCl \to PbCl_2\downarrow + 2NaNO_3$$

---

**Step 1:** Moles of each reactant.

> $\text{mol } Pb(NO_3)_2 = 0.250 \times 0.0350 = 0.00875 \text{ mol}$
>
> $\text{mol NaCl} = 0.180 \times 0.0500 = 0.00900 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Find limiting reagent.

> Divide by coefficients:
>
> $Pb(NO_3)_2$ ratio = $0.00875 / 1 = 0.00875$
>
> NaCl ratio = $0.00900 / 2 = 0.00450$
>
> $0.00450 < 0.00875 \;\to\;$ **NaCl is limiting.**

**Step 3:** Theoretical yield from limiting reagent.

> $\text{mol } PbCl_2 = 0.00900 \text{ mol NaCl} \times \dfrac{1 \text{ mol } PbCl_2}{2 \text{ mol NaCl}} = 0.00450 \text{ mol } PbCl_2$
>
> Molar mass $PbCl_2 = 207.2 + 2(35.45) = 278.10 \text{ g/mol}$
>
> $\text{g } PbCl_2 = 0.00450 \times 278.10 = 1.2515 \text{ g}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{1.25 \text{ g } PbCl_2 \text{ precipitate}}$ (3 sig figs).

This is the opening problem — solved.

---

## ⚠️ THE TRAP — Forgetting to Convert mL to L

**The wrong way:**

> 50.0 mL of 0.180 M NaCl. "mol = 0.180 × 50.0 = $\color{red}{9.00 \text{ mol}}$." ← **WRONG!**

**Why is this wrong?** Molarity is **moles per liter**, not moles per milliliter. 50.0 mL = 0.0500 L, not 50.0 L. Multiplying by mL gives an answer 1000× too large.

**The right way:**

> $\text{mol NaCl} = 0.180 \text{ mol/L} \times 0.0500 \text{ L} = 0.00900 \text{ mol}$. ✓

**Spot the difference:** $0.180 \times 50.0 = 9.00$ (wrong, off by 1000×) vs. $0.180 \times 0.0500 = 0.00900$ (right).

> **Mental checkpoint:** If your answer says there are 9 moles of something in a 50 mL test tube, that's physically absurd — 9 mol of NaCl would be about 526 g, more than half a kilogram in a tiny tube!

### Example 6 — Ion Concentrations in Solution

**Problem:** What is the concentration of each ion in 0.200 M $Na_2SO_4$ (sodium sulfate)?

---

**Step 1:** Write the dissociation equation.

> $Na_2SO_4 \to 2Na^+ + SO_4^{2-}$

**Step 2:** Use coefficients as multipliers.

> $[Na^+] = 2 \times 0.200 \text{ M} = 0.400 \text{ M}$
>
> $[SO_4^{2-}] = 1 \times 0.200 \text{ M} = 0.200 \text{ M}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{[Na^+] = 0.400 \text{ M},\; [SO_4^{2-}] = 0.200 \text{ M}}$.

---

### Example 7 — Preparing a Solution

**Problem:** How many grams of $CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$ (copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate) are needed to prepare 500.0 mL of 0.100 M $CuSO_4$?

---

**Step 1:** Moles of $CuSO_4$ needed.

> $\text{mol } CuSO_4 = 0.100 \text{ M} \times 0.5000 \text{ L} = 0.0500 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Molar mass of the hydrate.

> $CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$:
>
> Cu = 63.55, S = 32.07, O₄ = 64.00, 5H₂O = 5 × 18.016 = 90.08
>
> Total = 63.55 + 32.07 + 64.00 + 90.08 = 249.70 g/mol

**Step 3:** Mass needed.

> $\text{g} = 0.0500 \text{ mol} \times 249.70 \text{ g/mol} = 12.485 \text{ g}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{12.5 \text{ g } CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O}$.

> **Important:** When a hydrated salt is used, the water molecules are part of the molar mass! Don't forget them.

### Example 8 — Mixing Two Solutions: New Concentration

**Problem:** You mix 150.0 mL of 0.400 M NaCl with 250.0 mL of 0.200 M NaCl. What is the $[NaCl]$ in the mixture? What is $[Na^+]$?

---

**Step 1:** Total moles of NaCl.

> From first solution: $\text{mol} = 0.400 \times 0.1500 = 0.0600 \text{ mol}$
>
> From second solution: $\text{mol} = 0.200 \times 0.2500 = 0.0500 \text{ mol}$
>
> Total mol NaCl = $0.0600 + 0.0500 = 0.1100 \text{ mol}$

**Step 2:** Total volume.

> $V_{\text{total}} = 150.0 + 250.0 = 400.0 \text{ mL} = 0.4000 \text{ L}$

**Step 3:** New concentration.

> $[NaCl] = \dfrac{0.1100 \text{ mol}}{0.4000 \text{ L}} = 0.2750 \text{ M}$
>
> Since NaCl dissociates completely: $[Na^+] = [Cl^-] = 0.2750 \text{ M}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{[NaCl] = 0.275 \text{ M},\; [Na^+] = 0.275 \text{ M},\; [Cl^-] = 0.275 \text{ M}}$.

## What We Just Did — The Solution Stoichiometry Procedure

### Step 1: Convert solution information to MOLES.

> If given **molarity and volume:** $\text{mol} = M \times V(\text{L})$.
>
> **Always convert mL to L first** — divide by 1000.
>
> If given **mass and volume:** $\text{mol} = \text{g} \div \text{molar mass}$, then $M = \text{mol} / V(\text{L})$.

### Step 2: Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation.

> Exactly the same as Session 05 — the mole ratio doesn't care whether the reactants came from solids or solutions.
>
> If both reactants are in solution, find the limiting reagent using the method from Session 06.

### Step 3: Convert moles of target to the requested form.

> Grams? $\text{mol} \times \text{molar mass}$.
>
> Concentration? $\text{mol} / V_{\text{total}}(\text{L})$.
>
> Volume of a solution needed? $V(\text{L}) = \text{mol} / M$.

---

$$\text{mL} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,1000}\; \text{L} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,M}\; \text{mol A} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,\frac{\text{coeff B}}{\text{coeff A}}}\; \text{mol B} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,\text{MM}}\; \text{g B}$$

---

Copy this. The only new piece is the first arrow: mL → L → mol.

## 💡 Pro Tips — Calculator Shortcuts & Mental Checks

**Tip 1 — mL → L in your head.** Move the decimal 3 places left. $25.0 \text{ mL} = 0.0250 \text{ L}$. $500.0 \text{ mL} = 0.5000 \text{ L}$. The #1 mistake in solution stoichiometry is forgetting this — it makes your answer 1000× too large.

**Tip 2 — $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ works in mL.** The units cancel, so you don't need to convert to liters: $2.00 \times 50.0 = M_2 \times 200.0$ → $M_2 = 0.500$ M. Only convert to L when you need actual moles ($\text{mol} = M \times V(\text{L})$).

**Tip 3 — Dilution factor shortcut.** Final volume ÷ initial volume = dilution factor. $200/50 = 4$ → concentration drops by 4×. $2.00/4 = 0.500$ M. Instant answer without the formula.

**Tip 4 — Ion concentration = coefficient × compound concentration.** In $CaCl_2$: $[Cl^-] = 2 \times [CaCl_2]$. In $Al_2(SO_4)_3$: $[Al^{3+}] = 2 \times [Al_2(SO_4)_3]$, $[SO_4^{2-}] = 3 \times [Al_2(SO_4)_3]$. This is always true for complete dissociation.

**Tip 5 — Precipitate sanity check.** If both solutions are dilute (~0.1 M) and volumes are tens of mL, your precipitate mass should be in the **milligram to single-digit gram** range. If you calculate 500 g of precipitate from two 50 mL solutions, you forgot the mL → L conversion.

## 📝 Key Terms — Quick Reference

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **molarity (M)** | $\text{mol} / \text{L}$ — the standard concentration unit in chemistry |
| **solute** | the substance being dissolved (e.g., salt) |
| **solvent** | the substance doing the dissolving (usually water) |
| **dilution** | adding solvent to lower concentration; $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ |
| **mL → L** | divide by 1000 — the #1 mistake in solution problems |
| **$M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ in mL** | volumes can stay in mL (they cancel); only convert when finding moles |
| **precipitate** | insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed |
| **dissociation** | ionic compound splits into ions: $NaCl \to Na^+ + Cl^-$ |
| **spectator ion** | ion that doesn't participate in the reaction (e.g., $Na^+$ in many precipitations) |
| **ion concentration** | $[\text{ion}] = \text{coefficient} \times [\text{compound}]$ for complete dissociation |
