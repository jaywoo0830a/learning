## Worked Pattern

### Example 1 — Molar Mass from the Periodic Table

**Problem:** What is the molar mass of helium (He)? Of water ($H_2O$, "water")? Of calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$, "calcium carbonate")?

---

**Helium (He):**

From the periodic table: $\text{He} = 4.003 \text{ g/mol}$.
$\text{Molar mass} = 4.003 \text{ g/mol}$.

$\to$ One mole of helium atoms has a mass of 4.003 g.

---

**Water ($H_2O$):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{H: } &2 \times 1.008 = 2.016 \text{ g/mol} \\
\text{O: } &1 \times 16.00 = 16.00 \text{ g/mol} \\ \hline
\text{Total: } &2.016 + 16.00 = \mathbf{18.016 \text{ g/mol}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\to$ One mole of water molecules has a mass of 18.016 g.

---

**Calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$):**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Ca: } &1 \times 40.08 = 40.08 \text{ g/mol} \\
\text{C: } &1 \times 12.01 = 12.01 \text{ g/mol} \\
\text{O: } &3 \times 16.00 = 48.00 \text{ g/mol} \\ \hline
\text{Total: } &40.08 + 12.01 + 48.00 = \mathbf{100.09 \text{ g/mol}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\to$ One mole of $CaCO_3$ formula units has a mass of 100.09 g.

---

**Key insight:** Molar mass is the mass of $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles of that substance.

$$\boxed{\text{Molar mass has units of g/mol — always.}}$$

---

### Example 2 — Grams → Moles (One Step)

**Problem:** How many moles are in 50.0 g of $H_2O$?

**Step 1:** Find the molar mass.

> $H_2O = 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.016 \text{ g/mol}$

**Step 2:** Divide grams by molar mass.

> $\text{mol} = 50.0 \text{ g} \div 18.016 \text{ g/mol}$

**Step 3:** Calculate and apply sig figs.

> $50.0 \div 18.016 = 2.77531...$
>
> Sig figs: $50.0$ (3 sig figs) limits the answer.
>
> $\text{mol} = 2.78 \text{ mol } H_2O$

**Answer:** $\boxed{2.78 \text{ mol } H_2O}$.

$$
\underbrace{50.0\text{ g}}_{\text{grams}} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,18.016\text{ g/mol}}\; \underbrace{2.78\text{ mol}}_{\text{moles}} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,N_A}\; \underbrace{1.67 \times 10^{24}}_{\text{molecules}}
$$

This chain — grams $\to$ moles $\to$ particles — is the backbone of all stoichiometry.

---

### Example 3 — Moles → Grams (Reverse Direction)

**Problem:** What is the mass of 0.250 mol of $CO_2$ (carbon dioxide)?

**Step 1:** Find the molar mass.

> $CO_2 = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01 \text{ g/mol}$

**Step 2:** Multiply moles by molar mass.

> $\text{grams} = 0.250 \text{ mol} \times 44.01 \text{ g/mol}$

**Step 3:** Calculate.

> $0.250 \times 44.01 = 11.0025$
>
> Sig figs: $0.250$ (3 sig figs) limits the answer.
>
> $\text{mass} = 11.0 \text{ g } CO_2$

**Answer:** $\boxed{11.0 \text{ g } CO_2}$.

The direction signpost:

$$
\text{grams} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,\text{molar mass}}\; \text{moles} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,\text{molar mass}}\; \text{grams}
$$

$$
\text{g} \div \frac{\text{g}}{\text{mol}} = \text{mol} \qquad\qquad \text{mol} \times \frac{\text{g}}{\text{mol}} = \text{g}
$$

The unit cancellation tells you which operation is correct.

---

### Example 4 — Moles → Number of Particles

**Problem:** How many atoms are in 2.00 mol of helium?

**Step 1:** Recall Avogadro's number.

> $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ particles/mol}$
>
> "1 mole of anything contains $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ of those things."

**Step 2:** Multiply moles by Avogadro's number.

> $\text{atoms} = 2.00 \text{ mol} \times (6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms/mol})$

**Step 3:** Calculate.

> $2.00 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 1.2044 \times 10^{24}$
>
> Sig figs: $2.00$ (3 sig figs).
>
> $\text{atoms} = 1.20 \times 10^{24} \text{ He atoms}$

**Answer:** $\boxed{1.20 \times 10^{24} \text{ He atoms}}$.

$$
\text{mol} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,6.022\times10^{23}}\; \text{particles}
$$

---

### Example 5 — Number of Particles → Moles

**Problem:** A sample contains $3.01 \times 10^{23}$ molecules of $O_2$ (oxygen gas). How many moles is this?

**Step 1:** Divide number of particles by Avogadro's number.

> $\text{mol} = (3.01 \times 10^{23} \text{ particles}) \div (6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ particles/mol})$

**Step 2:** Calculate.

> $3.01 \times 10^{23} \div 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \dfrac{3.01}{6.022} = 0.49983...$
>
> The $10^{23}$ cancels cleanly.

**Step 3:** Apply sig figs.

> $3.01$ (3 sig figs).
>
> $\text{mol} = 0.500 \text{ mol } O_2$

**Answer:** $\boxed{0.500 \text{ mol } O_2}$.

$\therefore$ $3.01 \times 10^{23}$ is exactly $\frac{1}{2} N_A$ — half a mole. The numbers confirm it.

## ⚠️ THE TRAP — Using the Wrong Molar Mass for Diatomic Gases

**The wrong way:**

> How many moles are in 32.0 g of oxygen gas?
>
> *Wrong work:* Molar mass of O = 16.00 g/mol.
> mol = 32.0 ÷ 16.00 = $\color{red}{2.00 \text{ mol}}$ ← **WRONG!**

**Why is this wrong?** Oxygen gas exists as $O_2$ molecules, not isolated O atoms. The substance in the problem is $O_2$, so you must use the molar mass of $O_2$: $2 \times 16.00 = 32.00 \text{ g/mol}$.

**The right way:**

**Step 1:** Identify the substance. "Oxygen gas" = $O_2$, NOT $O$.

> Molar mass of $O_2 = 2 \times 16.00 = 32.00 \text{ g/mol}$.

**Step 2:** Divide grams by molar mass.

> $\text{mol} = 32.0 \text{ g} \div 32.00 \text{ g/mol} = 1.00 \text{ mol } O_2$

**Step 3:** Verify: $32.0 \text{ g}$ of $O_2$ is $1.00 \text{ mol}$ of $O_2$ molecules.

> If you needed mol of O atoms: $1.00 \text{ mol } O_2 \times 2 = 2.00 \text{ mol O atoms}$.

**Spot the difference:** "Oxygen gas" means $O_2$ (molar mass = 32.00 g/mol). "Oxygen atoms" means O (molar mass = 16.00 g/mol). The diatomic elements are: $H_2, N_2, O_2, F_2, Cl_2, Br_2, I_2$. Always use the molecular form for these when they appear as pure substances.

### Example 6 — The Full Chain: Grams → Moles → Number of Atoms

**Problem (the opening attack):** Which contains more atoms: 10.0 g of helium or 10.0 g of calcium?

---

**Helium path:**

> **Step 1:** $\text{mol He} = 10.0 \text{ g} \div 4.003 \text{ g/mol} = 2.4981 \text{ mol He}$
>
> **Step 2:** $\text{atoms He} = 2.4981 \text{ mol} \times (6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms/mol}) = 1.504 \times 10^{24} \text{ He atoms}$

**Calcium path:**

> **Step 1:** $\text{mol Ca} = 10.0 \text{ g} \div 40.08 \text{ g/mol} = 0.24950 \text{ mol Ca}$
>
> **Step 2:** $\text{atoms Ca} = 0.24950 \text{ mol} \times (6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms/mol}) = 1.502 \times 10^{23} \text{ Ca atoms}$

---

**Answer:** $\boxed{1.50 \times 10^{24} \text{ He atoms}}$ — **10 times more** than $\boxed{1.50 \times 10^{23} \text{ Ca atoms}}$.

$$
\underbrace{10.0\text{ g He}}_{\text{same mass}} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,4.003}\; \underbrace{2.50\text{ mol}}_{\text{10× more moles!}} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,N_A}\; 1.50 \times 10^{24} \text{ atoms}
$$

$$
\underbrace{10.0\text{ g Ca}}_{\text{same mass}} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,40.08}\; \underbrace{0.250\text{ mol}}_{\text{10× fewer moles}} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,N_A}\; 1.50 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms}
$$

$$
\therefore\; \text{He wins by a factor of } \frac{40.08}{4.003} \approx 10
$$

### Example 7 — Molecules vs. Atoms Within a Compound

**Problem:** How many oxygen atoms are in 50.0 g of $CaCO_3$?

**Step 1:** $\text{mol } CaCO_3 = 50.0 \text{ g} \div 100.09 \text{ g/mol} = 0.49955 \text{ mol } CaCO_3$

**Step 2:** Each $CaCO_3$ formula unit contains 3 oxygen atoms.

> $\text{mol O atoms} = 0.49955 \text{ mol } CaCO_3 \times 3 = 1.4987 \text{ mol O atoms}$

**Step 3:** $\text{atoms O} = 1.4987 \text{ mol} \times (6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ atoms/mol}) = 9.025 \times 10^{23} \text{ O atoms}$

> Sig figs: $50.0$ (3 sig figs) $\to$ $9.02 \times 10^{23}$ O atoms

**Answer:** $\boxed{9.02 \times 10^{23} \text{ oxygen atoms}}$.

$$
\text{grams } CaCO_3 \;\xrightarrow{\div\,100.09}\; \text{mol } CaCO_3 \;\xrightarrow{\times\,3}\; \text{mol O atoms} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,N_A}\; \text{O atoms}
$$

The subscript is a mole ratio too: $1\text{ mol } CaCO_3 : 3\text{ mol O}$.

---

### Example 8 — Mass of a Single Atom/Molecule

**Problem:** What is the mass (in grams) of a single water molecule?

**Step 1:** Molar mass of $H_2O = 18.016 \text{ g/mol}$.

> This is the mass of $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ water molecules.

**Step 2:** Divide by Avogadro's number.

> $\text{mass of 1 molecule} = 18.016 \text{ g/mol} \div 6.022 \times 10^{23} \text{ molecules/mol}$
>
> $\phantom{\text{mass of 1 molecule}} = 2.9917 \times 10^{-23} \text{ g}$

**Step 3:** Apply sig figs.

> $18.016$ (5 sig figs) limits the answer.
>
> $\text{mass} = 2.9917 \times 10^{-23} \text{ g per molecule}$

**Answer:** $\boxed{2.992 \times 10^{-23} \text{ g per } H_2O \text{ molecule}}$.

$$
\frac{18.016\text{ g}}{\cancel{\text{mol}}} \times \frac{1\;\cancel{\text{mol}}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ molecules}} = 2.992 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g/molecule}
$$

That is $0.00000000000000000000002992$ g — unimaginably tiny. Avogadro's number is the bridge from this microscopic world to the gram quantities we can weigh on a balance.

## What We Just Did — The Mole Conversion Procedure

### Step 1: Identify your starting point and your destination.

> **Starting:** grams? particles? moles?
>
> **Destination:** grams? particles? moles?
>
> This tells you which direction to go.

### Step 2: Use the correct conversion factor.

> **grams $\leftrightarrow$ moles:** use molar mass (g/mol) from the periodic table.
>
> **moles $\leftrightarrow$ particles:** use Avogadro's number ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles/mol).
>
> **Going grams $\to$ moles $\to$ particles:** chain both conversions.

### Step 3: Let the units guide you.

> $\text{g} \div (\text{g/mol}) = \text{mol}$ ✓
>
> $\text{mol} \times (\text{g/mol}) = \text{g}$ ✓
>
> $\text{mol} \times (\text{particles/mol}) = \text{particles}$ ✓
>
> If the units don't cancel to your destination, you used the wrong operation.

$$\text{grams} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,M}\; \text{moles} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,N_A}\; \text{particles}$$

$$\text{particles} \;\xrightarrow{\div\,N_A}\; \text{moles} \;\xrightarrow{\times\,M}\; \text{grams}$$

where $M$ = molar mass (g/mol) and $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles/mol.

---

Copy this. The mole is the central hub — every road from grams to particles passes through it.

## 💡 Pro Tips — Calculator Shortcuts & Mental Checks

**Tip 1 — Grams ↔ Moles direction.** If the number you're converting is **bigger than the molar mass**, you have **more than 1 mole**. If smaller, less than 1 mole. This sanity check catches most calculator errors.

> $50.0 \text{ g } H_2O$ ÷ $18.016 \text{ g/mol}$ → must be < 3 mol (since $3 \times 18 = 54 > 50$). Actual: 2.78 mol. ✓

**Tip 2 — Diatomic mnemonic.** "**H**ave **N**o **F**ear **O**f **I**ce **C**old **Br**ew" → $H_2, N_2, F_2, O_2, I_2, Cl_2, Br_2$. If the problem says "oxygen gas" or "nitrogen gas" and you forgot to double the molar mass, your moles will be off by a factor of 2.

**Tip 3 — Avogadro shortcuts.** Half a mole = $3.011 \times 10^{23}$. A tenth of a mole = $6.022 \times 10^{22}$. These round numbers appear on multiple-choice questions constantly.

**Tip 4 — Molar mass of common compounds.** Memorize these — they appear in nearly every problem: $H_2O = 18.02$, $CO_2 = 44.01$, $NaCl = 58.44$, $CaCO_3 = 100.1$, $NH_3 = 17.03$, $O_2 = 32.00$, $H_2 = 2.02$ g/mol.

**Tip 5 — Calculator hygiene.** Keep all 10 digits on your calculator screen until the very last step. Rounding intermediate values (e.g., rounding mol from 2.7758 to 2.78 before multiplying by $N_A$) can shift your final answer by $10^{21}$ particles. Only round the final answer.

**Tip 6 — 1 amu = 1 g/mol.** The number on the periodic table is BOTH the mass of one atom in atomic mass units AND the mass of one mole in grams. Carbon-12: 1 atom = 12 amu, 1 mol = 12 g. Same number, two different scales. This is not a coincidence — the mole was defined so that grams and amu line up perfectly. So when you read "55.85" for iron, you instantly know: 1 atom = 55.85 amu, 1 mol = 55.85 g.

## 📝 Key Terms — Quick Reference

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **mole (mol)** | $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ things — the chemist's "dozen" |
| **Avogadro's number ($N_A$)** | $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles/mol |
| **molar mass ($M$)** | mass of 1 mole in g/mol; found on the periodic table |
| **grams → moles** | divide by molar mass: $\text{mol} = \text{g} \div M$ |
| **moles → grams** | multiply by molar mass: $\text{g} = \text{mol} \times M$ |
| **moles → particles** | multiply by $N_A$ |
| **particles → moles** | divide by $N_A$ |
| **diatomic elements** | $H_2, N_2, O_2, F_2, Cl_2, Br_2, I_2$ — always use molecular molar mass |
| **formula unit** | the smallest repeating unit of an ionic compound (e.g., NaCl, not a molecule) |
| **molar mass of hydrate** | includes the mass of water molecules ($CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$ = 249.7 g/mol) |
