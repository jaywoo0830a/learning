## Problem 1 — The Gravity Analogy

**1.1 —** A positive charge released in a field moves **toward lower potential** (the field points from high to low $V$). This is exactly a ball rolling **downhill** — potential plays the role of height.

**1.2 —** **You** must do work to push it against the field (the field does negative work on it), and its electric potential energy **increases** — like lifting a ball up a hill.

**1.3 —** $KE=q\Delta V=(2.0\times10^{-6})(500)=1.0\times10^{-3}$ J $=1$ mJ. Released from rest, that's its full kinetic energy gain.

> **The feel:** potential is "electric height." Charges spontaneously roll downhill (to lower $V$), and the energy gained is $q$ times the height dropped — the exact analogue of $mgh$.

## Problem 2 — Voltage as Energy per Charge

**2.1 —** $9$ V $=9$ J per coulomb. Voltage is *energy per charge*, not "amount of electricity."

**2.2 —** $U=qV=(3.0)(9)=27$ J.

**2.3 —** $U=qV=(2.0\times10^{-6})(500)=1.0\times10^{-3}$ J $=1.0$ mJ.

> **The feel:** a battery label tells you how much energy each coulomb of charge will deliver. To get the total energy you always multiply voltage by the charge that actually moves.

## Problem 3 — Equipotential Feeling

**3.1 —** **Zero.** Moving along an equipotential means $\Delta V=0$, so the field does $W=q\Delta V=0$ work — like walking along a contour line on a hill, where your height never changes.

**3.2 —** The field points from **high to low potential** (downhill), and it is **perpendicular** to equipotential lines — exactly like the steepest descent on a topographic map being perpendicular to the contour lines.

**3.3 —** $V=U/q$ is the potential energy **per unit charge** — a property of the point (measured in volts). $U=qV$ is the actual energy of a specific charge at that point. Same relation as $h$ (height) vs $mgh$ (energy of a specific mass).

> **The feel:** equipotentials are "height contours" for electric potential. Moving along one costs nothing; crossing them is where energy changes.

---

## Problem 4 — The "Voltage is Force" Misconception

**4.1 —** No — with no potential difference there's no energy change, so a charge sitting there has no reason to move toward the other point. What drives motion is a **potential difference** (voltage), not potential itself.

**4.2 —** **Current** (charge) flows through the wire; **voltage** is what drives it — like the height difference that drives water through a pipe. Voltage doesn't "flow" anywhere; it's the pressure that pushes the flow.

**4.3 —** $9$ V means the battery delivers **9 joules per coulomb** of energy. Total energy $=V\times q$: a battery's stored energy depends on both its voltage and how much charge it can push.

> **The misconception fixed:** voltage is not a thing that flows, nor is it force. It's the *energy-per-charge* difference that drives charge to move — the electric cousin of a height difference.

## Problem 5 — The "High Voltage = More Energy" Trap

**5.1 —** Case A: $U=qV=(1.0\times10^{-6})(10{,}000)=0.010$ J. Case B: $U=(100\times10^{-6})(1.0)=0.00010$ J. **Case A transfers 100× more energy** — a tiny charge through a huge voltage beats a huge charge through a tiny voltage here.

**5.2 —** Energy is $U=qV$: you must know **both** the voltage and the charge. "High voltage" is only half the story.

**5.3 —** A static shock has thousands of volts but a **tiny charge**, so $U=qV$ is only microjoules — enough for a sting, not damage. A $12$ V battery has a small voltage but can drive a **huge total charge**, so the energy it can deliver is large — that's why a shorted car battery can be dangerous. Voltage alone never tells you the energy; charge matters.

> **The misconception fixed:** "high voltage = high energy/danger" ignores the charge factor. The real quantity is $U=qV$ — both factors count, and everyday statics have lots of $V$ but almost no $q$.
