## Problem 1 — The Coulomb's Law Feeling

**1.1 —** $F=k\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}=9.0\times10^9\times\frac{(1\times10^{-6})(2\times10^{-6})}{(0.10)^2}=1.8$ N (repulsive — both positive).

**1.2 —** $F\propto 1/r^2$: doubling $r$ gives $F/4=0.45$ N.

**1.3 —** Triple one charge (×3) and halve the distance (×4): net factor $3\times4=12$, so $F=1.8\times12=21.6$ N. Distance wins — it's squared.

> **The feel:** the inverse square is the whole game. Halving the distance quadruples the force; doubling it quarters the force. This is the same law as gravity — Coulomb just swaps mass for charge.

## Problem 2 — The Field as "Force per Charge"

**2.1 —** $E=F/q=6.0/(2.0\times10^{-6})=3.0\times10^6$ N/C.

**2.2 —** $F=qE=(5.0\times10^{-6})(3.0\times10^6)=15$ N. The **field is the same**; only the test charge changed. Force scales with the charge sitting in the field.

**2.3 —** $E=F/q$ is force per unit charge, exactly like $g=F/m$ is force per unit mass. The "test object" is a small charge for $E$ and a small mass for $g$. Both fields are **properties of the space** — they exist even with no test object placed there.

> **The feel:** just as $g$ tells you "how strong gravity is here" regardless of what you drop, $E$ tells you "how strong the electric push is here" regardless of what charge you bring.

## Problem 3 — Superposition of Fields

**3.1 —** **Zero.** The two equal charges push in opposite directions at the midpoint with equal magnitudes; the fields cancel exactly.

**3.2 —** Both fields now point **toward the negative charge** (fields point into $-$), so they add: **net $=2E_1$**, pointing from the $+$ toward the $-$ charge.

**3.3 —** (a) Single $+$: lines **radiate outward**, starting on the charge and going to infinity. (b) Dipole: lines leave the $+$ charge and **end on the $-$ charge**. Field lines never cross, and their density shows field strength.

> **The feel:** fields are vectors that add like vectors — the mechanics of vector addition you already know applies directly. Symmetric setups often cancel (that's the first thing to check).

## Problem 4 — The Field Direction Misconception

**4.1 —** A positive charge is pushed **right** (along $E$); a negative charge is pushed **left** (opposite $E$). By definition, $E$ points the way a *positive* charge would be pushed.

**4.2 —** $E=F/q$ is the force **per unit charge** — the definition divides by the test charge so that $E$ doesn't depend on which test charge you use. The field exists at the point even with no charge there; it's "force per charge of the space," not "the force on this particular charge."

**4.3 —** Field lines show the **direction of the force at each point**, not the path. As a charge moves, the force keeps changing direction, so its actual path curves and generally does **not** follow the field lines (except in special symmetric cases like straight-line motion along a uniform field).

> **The misconception fixed:** $E$ is a property of space (like $g$), not of the charge in it. And field lines are a map of force *directions*, not train tracks.

## Problem 5 — The Inverse-Square Trap

**5.1 —** $F/9$ — the distance tripled, so $3^2=9$.

**5.2 —** Halving the distance quadruples the force: $F=40\times4=160$ N.

**5.3 —** A point source spreads its influence over the surface of an imaginary sphere of radius $r$; the surface area is $4\pi r^2$, so the "intensity" per area falls as $1/r^2$. That's why both gravity and electric force obey inverse square — they radiate through space. Contact forces like friction act at a surface and don't spread, so they have no $1/r^2$ behavior.

> **The misconception fixed:** "double distance = half force" is the intuitive but wrong guess. It's actually *quarter* the force — the same reason a light bulb looks 4× dimmer when you double your distance from it.
