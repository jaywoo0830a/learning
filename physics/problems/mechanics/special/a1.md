# Mechanics — Special A1 — The Untrained Four

> **Mix:** Circular Motion · Linear Momentum · Rotation of a Rigid Body · Torque & Machines — **5 problems per topic, 20 total.**
> **Status:** *Special session* — a focused supplement outside the numbered day rotation, for topics not yet drilled: Circular Motion, Linear Momentum, Rotation, Torque & Machines.
> **Level:** Regular Physics / SAT Physics — **algebraic only, no calculus.** Every problem uses kinematic equations, $F=ma$, conservation laws, and rotational analogues ($\tau=I\alpha$, $L=I\omega$, $KE=\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, $v=\omega r$).
> **How to use:** Draw a diagram first. Name the principle before each step ("Newton's 2nd law, radial direction", "conservation of angular momentum", "torque balance about the pivot"). Keep units on every number. Solve symbolically, then plug numbers at the end. **Solve all 20 before opening the solutions file.**
> **Deliberately hint-free:** the principle and strategy for each problem are *not* stated — recalling them is part of the exercise. If you peek, you have already lost part of the training.

# Part A — Circular Motion

## Problem A1 — The Tilted String

A small bob of mass $m=0.50$ kg is tied to a string of length $L=2.0$ m and swung so that it moves in a **horizontal circle** with the string making a fixed angle $\theta=37°$ with the vertical. Take $\sin37°=0.60$, $\cos37°=0.80$, $\tan37°=0.75$.

**(a)** Draw the free-body diagram. Name the force that provides the centripetal force, and find the tension $T$ in the string.

**(b)** Find the speed $v$ of the bob and the radius $r$ of its circular path.

**(c)** Find the period of the motion in terms of $L$, $g$, and $\theta$. What does the period approach as $\theta\to0°$? What famous result does that match?

**(d)** As $\theta\to90°$, the bob swings in a nearly horizontal circle of radius $\approx L$. Use your expression for $T$ and $v$ to explain what happens to the tension and the speed, and why the string would snap long before $\theta=90°$ is reached.

## Problem A2 — The Rotor That Never Drops

In an amusement-park "Rotor," a person of mass $m=60$ kg stands against the wall of a spinning cylinder of radius $R=3.0$ m. The coefficient of static friction between the person and the wall is $\mu_s=0.40$. Once the cylinder spins fast enough, the floor drops away and the person does **not** slide down.

**(a)** Draw the force diagram. Which force supports the person's weight? Which force is centripetal?

**(b)** Find the minimum angular speed $\omega_{\min}$ (and the corresponding period) for which the person stays pinned to the wall.

**(c)** At $\omega_{\min}$, find the normal force the wall exerts on the person. Express it as a multiple of the person's weight $mg$. What do you notice about your result?

**(d)** If the ride's designers double the coefficient of friction (new rubber wall, $\mu_s=0.80$), by what factor does $\omega_{\min}$ change? Explain your result in one sentence.

## Problem A3 — The Curve With an Attitude

A car of mass $1200$ kg rounds a curve of radius $R=50$ m.

**(a)** On a **flat** curve with $\mu_s=0.60$, find the maximum speed before it skids.

**(b)** The curve is rebuilt as a **frictionless banked** curve designed for a speed of about $20$ m/s. Find the bank angle $\theta$ (give both $\tan\theta$ and $\theta$).

**(c)** Now the same banked curve is given a surface with $\mu_s=0.50$. Find the **range** of speeds — a maximum and a minimum — for which the car can round the curve without sliding.

**(d)** On the original **flat** curve, it starts to rain and $\mu_s$ drops to $0.30$. By what factor does the maximum safe speed drop? How does this compare with the banked case, which needs no friction at the design speed?

## Problem A4 — The Ball That Swings in a Circle

A ball of mass $m=2.0$ kg is tied to a light string of length $L=1.5$ m and whirled in a **vertical circle**.

**(a)** At the bottom of the circle its speed is $v_b=9.0$ m/s. Find the tension at the bottom, then the speed at the top, and the tension there.

**(b)** Find the **minimum** speed the ball needs at the top for the string to stay taut, and the corresponding speed at the bottom.

**(c)** Instead, the ball is released from rest with the string **horizontal** (the ball starts at the side of the circle). Find its speed at the bottom and the tension there. Express the tension in terms of $mg$ — what pattern do you see, and does $L$ matter?

**(d)** Generalize: the ball is released from rest when the string makes angle $\theta_0$ with the vertical (measured from the bottom). Derive the tension at the bottom in terms of $m$, $g$, and $\theta_0$. Check your formula against the limiting cases $\theta_0=90°$ and $\theta_0=0°$, and interpret what each case represents. Then compute it for $\theta_0=120°$ (the ball starts $30°$ above the horizontal).

## Problem A5 — The Hill, the Dip, and the Feeling in Your Stomach

A car of mass $m=1200$ kg drives at $v=10$ m/s over a **circular hump** of radius $R=30$ m (like the crest of a hill), then through a **circular dip** of the same radius.

**(a)** Draw the force diagram at the top of the hump. Find the normal force $N$ there.

**(b)** The driver speeds up. Find the speed at which the car **loses contact** with the hump ($N=0$). What does the driver feel at that moment?

**(c)** Now the car goes through the dip at the same $v=10$ m/s. Find the normal force at the bottom of the dip.

**(d)** Where is the driver heaviest — over the hump or through the dip? Where is the lightest? Use your answers to (a) and (c) to explain, and state the condition for "weightlessness" over the hump.

# Part B — Linear Momentum

## Problem B1 — The Cannon That Punches Back

A cannon of mass $M=800$ kg sits on a flat surface. It fires a shell of mass $m=10$ kg with a muzzle speed $v=300$ m/s **relative to the ground**, measured just as the shell leaves the barrel.

**(a)** Find the recoil speed $V$ of the cannon. Name the principle.

**(b)** The cannon is now aimed at $30°$ above the horizontal. Find the new recoil speed. What carries the vertical momentum of the shell?

**(c)** With the cannon's wheels locked so it drags on the ground with $\mu_k=0.20$, find how far the cannon slides before stopping after the horizontal shot of part (a).

**(d)** The barrel is $L=2.0$ m long, and the shell accelerates roughly uniformly from rest to $300$ m/s while inside it. Find the average force the propellant gas exerts on the shell, and the impulse delivered. Check: does the impulse equal the shell's momentum change?



---

## Problem B2 — The Firework That Divides

A firework shell of mass $M=3.0$ kg is launched straight up. At the **top of its flight** (where its speed is momentarily zero) it explodes into two pieces: $m_1=1.0$ kg and $m_2=2.0$ kg. Piece 1 flies off horizontally to the right at $v_1=40$ m/s. The explosion height is $h=80$ m.

**(a)** Find the velocity (speed and direction) of piece 2 immediately after the explosion. Name the principle and explain why it applies even though chemical energy is released.

**(b)** Compute the total kinetic energy of the two pieces **after** the explosion. How much kinetic energy was there **before**? Where did the extra energy come from?

**(c)** How long does it take each piece to reach the ground from height $h$, and how far from directly below the explosion point does each piece land?

**(d)** Suppose the shell instead breaks into **three** equal pieces (1.0 kg each): piece A flies $+x$ at $30$ m/s and piece B flies $+y$ at $40$ m/s. Find piece C's velocity (magnitude and direction). What is special about the three momenta as vectors?



---

## Problem B3 — The Bullet and the Block on Strings

A bullet of mass $m=0.020$ kg is fired horizontally into a wooden block of mass $M=2.00$ kg hanging at rest from two light strings of length $L=1.0$ m. The bullet embeds in the block, and the block+bullet swing up to a maximum height $h=0.20$ m.

**(a)** Find the speed of the block+bullet immediately after the collision.

**(b)** Find the speed of the bullet **just before** impact.

**(c)** Compute the kinetic energy before and after the collision, and the fraction of energy lost. Where does it go?

**(d)** Now suppose the bullet instead **bounces back** at one quarter of its incoming speed (a rubber bullet, same incoming speed). Find the block's speed right after, and the new maximum height. Compare with (a)/(b) and explain why the block rises higher.



---

## Problem B4 — The Perfect Two-Dimensional Collision

A puck of mass $m=0.50$ kg moving on a frictionless table at $v_0=5.0$ m/s hits an **identical stationary** puck. After the collision, puck 1 moves off at $37°$ above its original direction with speed $v_1=4.0$ m/s. ($\sin37°=0.60$, $\cos37°=0.80$.)

**(a)** Write the two momentum-conservation equations (along $x$ and along $y$). Use them to find the speed $v_2$ and direction $\theta_2$ of puck 2's motion.

**(b)** Show that the collision is **elastic**.

**(c)** What is the angle between the two final velocities? Show your reasoning.

**(d)** **Proof challenge:** prove that for ANY elastic collision between two equal masses where one is initially at rest, the two final velocities are always perpendicular.



---

## Problem B5 — The Train That Couples, Couples, Couples

A freight car of mass $m=1.0\times10^4$ kg rolls toward a line of identical cars at rest, speed $v_0=3.0$ m/s. When it hits the first stationary car, the two couple (stick). The pair then rolls on and couples to the next stationary car, and so on. Neglect rolling friction.

**(a)** Find the speed of the pair right after the first coupling. Then the speed of the three-car train after the second coupling. Spot the pattern.

**(b)** Prove the pattern you spotted: find a general expression for the train's speed after $n$ cars have been coupled.

**(c)** What fraction of the original kinetic energy remains after all $5$ cars have coupled? How much energy (in joules) was lost altogether, and into what forms?

**(d)** Show that the same fraction of the kinetic energy present before a coupling is destroyed by each coupling, regardless of which coupling it is, and determine that fraction. Why does the result make sense?

# Part C — Rotation of a Rigid Body

## Problem C1 — The Flywheel That Stores Energy

A solid disk (mass $M=2.0$ kg, radius $R=0.50$ m, $I=\tfrac12MR^2$) is mounted on a frictionless axle and starts from rest. A constant torque $\tau=5.0$ N·m is applied for $t=10$ s.

**(a)** Find the moment of inertia and the angular acceleration $\alpha$.

**(b)** Find the angular speed $\omega$ after $10$ s and the total angle turned in revolutions.

**(c)** Find the rotational kinetic energy at $t=10$ s.

**(d)** Compute the work done by the torque and compare with (c). State the rotational work–energy theorem, and name the linear-motion quantity each rotational quantity plays the role of.



---

## Problem C2 — Two Disks, One Spin

Disk 1, with moment of inertia $I_1=0.40$ kg·m², spins freely at $\omega_1=20$ rad/s on a frictionless axle. A second disk, $I_2=0.60$ kg·m², initially at rest, is dropped onto the same axle and the two lock together (like a clutch engaging).

**(a)** Find the final angular speed $\omega_f$. Name the principle.

**(b)** Compute the kinetic energy before and after, and the fraction lost. Compare with the successive couplings in **Problem B5** — what is the same, what is different?

**(c)** Repeat (a) if disk 2 was initially spinning the **opposite** way at $10$ rad/s. In which direction does the pair spin now?

**(d)** Explain physically where the "lost" rotational kinetic energy goes. If the coupling were perfectly elastic (e.g., a frictionless magnetic coupling with no dissipation), what would be different?



---

## Problem C3 — The Race Down the Hill

A hoop, a solid disk, and a solid sphere — each of mass $m=2.0$ kg and radius $R=0.10$ m — start from rest at the top of an incline of height $h=1.0$ m and angle $\theta=30°$ and roll without slipping. ($I_{\text{hoop}}=mR^2$, $I_{\text{disk}}=\tfrac12mR^2$, $I_{\text{sphere}}=\tfrac25mR^2$.)

**(a)** Find the bottom speed of each body. Set up your energy accounting explicitly before plugging in numbers.

**(b)** Who wins the race — hoop, disk, or sphere? Explain in terms of where the energy goes. Why does the *mass* and *radius* of each body not matter?

**(c)** If the incline were **frictionless**, the bodies would slide without rotating. Find that speed and compare with the rolling speeds. Why does friction *slow the bodies down* even though it never does any work?

**(d)** For the disk rolling down the incline, find the **minimum** coefficient of static friction needed to prevent slipping. Repeat for the hoop and sphere — which body needs the grippiest surface?

## Problem C4 — The Falling Rod

A uniform rod of mass $m=3.0$ kg and length $L=2.0$ m is pivoted at one end. It is held **horizontal** and released from rest. ($I_{\text{about end}}=\tfrac13mL^2$.)

**(a)** Find the rod's moment of inertia about the pivot.

**(b)** Just after release, find the angular acceleration $\alpha$. Explain carefully how you chose the lever arm for the weight.

**(c)** Find the angular speed $\omega$ when the rod swings through the **vertical** position.

**(d)** Find the speed of the **tip** of the rod at the bottom. Compare with the speed a point mass would reach falling freely through the same height $L$. Which is larger, and why?

## Problem C5 — The Skater, the Platform, and Where the Energy Comes From

**(a)** A figure skater spins with arms extended: $I_1=4.0$ kg·m² at $\omega_1=2.0$ rad/s. She pulls her arms in, reducing her moment of inertia to $I_2=1.0$ kg·m². Find her new angular speed.

**(b)** Compute her rotational kinetic energy before and after. It **increases** — where does the extra energy come from? There is no external torque, yet energy is not conserved; explain why conservation of angular momentum and conservation of energy are different statements.

**(c)** A turntable of moment of inertia $I_p=20$ kg·m² rotates freely at $\omega_0=3.0$ rad/s. A student of mass $m=60$ kg stands at the rim, $r=1.0$ m from the axis (treat as a point mass, $I=mr^2$). The student walks slowly to the center. Find the final angular speed.

**(d)** Compute the kinetic energy of the turntable+student system before and after the walk. Where did the increase come from? Then answer: what would happen if the student walked back out to the rim — and what does that tell you about whether the student's muscles gain or lose energy?

# Part D — Torque & Machines

## Problem D1 — The Seesaw That Asks Questions

A uniform board of length $L=4.0$ m and mass $m_b=10$ kg is balanced on a pivot at its **center**. Child A ($m_A=25$ kg) sits at the left end; child B ($m_B=20$ kg) sits at the right end.

**(a)** Compute the net torque about the pivot (take counterclockwise positive). Which side goes down? Where does the board's own weight act, and what role does it play in the balance?

**(b)** To balance, one child must move. Keep B at the end and find where A must sit. Then answer the same if instead A stays at the end and B moves — is that possible? Explain.

**(c)** With A now sitting at the position found in (b), a third child C ($m_C=10$ kg) climbs onto the **right end**. Which way does it tip, and where must A move to restore balance?

**(d)** Now suppose the board is **not uniform**: its center of mass is displaced from the geometric center. With A at the left end and B at the right end, how far from the pivot (and on which side) must the board's center of mass lie for the system to balance exactly? *(Board mass is still $10$ kg.)*

## Problem D2 — The Wheelbarrow That Multiplies Force

A wheelbarrow carries a load of $W=600$ N. The load sits $a=0.40$ m in front of the wheel (the fulcrum), and the handles are $b=1.6$ m from the wheel.

**(a)** Find the upward force you must exert on the handles to just balance (lift) the load. Name the principle.

**(b)** Find the mechanical advantage $MA$ and verify it equals $W/F$. Find its value.

**(c)** The wheelbarrow itself has mass $10$ kg with its center of gravity $0.60$ m from the wheel. Find the new lifting force.

**(d)** To make it easier, the worker shifts the load so it sits $0.30$ m from the wheel. Find the new lifting force (ignore the body weight). Which design change — shortening the load distance by $0.10$ m, or lengthening the handles by $0.20$ m — reduces the force more? Show both and compare.

## Problem D3 — The Ladder That Wants to Slip

A uniform ladder of mass $m=15$ kg and length $L=5.0$ m leans against a **frictionless** vertical wall. The foot of the ladder is $3.0$ m from the wall, so the top rests against the wall $4.0$ m up (a 3–4–5 triangle). The floor has static friction $\mu_s$.

**(a)** Draw the force diagram. Find the normal force from the wall $N_w$, the normal force from the floor $N_g$, and the friction force $f$ at the foot.

**(b)** Find the minimum $\mu_s$ so the ladder does not slip.

**(c)** A person of mass $m_p=75$ kg climbs the ladder. With $\mu_s=0.375$, how far up the ladder (distance measured along the ladder from the foot) can they climb before it slips?

**(d)** The floor gets wet, $\mu_s$ drops to $0.20$. How far up can the same person climb now? Compare with (c) and comment on why the allowed height collapses so sharply.

## Problem D4 — The Pulley System That Shares the Load

A load of $W=400$ N is to be lifted with a system of light, frictionless pulleys.

**(a)** **One fixed pulley** (attached to the ceiling, rope over it, load on one end, you pull the other). Find the force $F$ you must pull with, and the mechanical advantage. What is the point of this pulley if it doesn't reduce the force?

**(b)** **One movable pulley** (rope fixed to the ceiling, passed under a pulley that carries the load, free end pulled up). Find $F$ and $MA$.

**(c)** **A block-and-tackle** in which the movable block is supported by **four** strands of rope. Find $F$ and $MA$. Explain how you decided the mechanical advantage.

**(d)** In reality each pulley has friction, so the system is only $80\%$ efficient (work out $=0.80\times$ work in). Find the actual force needed with the four-strand system. If you pulled the rope $4.0$ m, how high does the load rise (ideal), and how much work is done on the load versus by you?

## Problem D5 — Gears, Cranks, and the Winch

**(a)** Two meshed gears: the driver gear has radius $r_1=0.10$ m and $N_1=20$ teeth; the driven gear has $r_2=0.25$ m and $N_2=50$ teeth. The driver is driven by a torque $\tau_1=8.0$ N·m at $\omega_1=40$ rad/s. Find the contact force between the teeth, the torque on the driven gear, and the mechanical advantage. Compare your result with the tooth counts $N_1$ and $N_2$.

**(b)** Find the angular speed $\omega_2$ of the driven gear. Then verify that power is conserved. Why must this be true for an ideal (frictionless) gear train?

**(c)** A **winch** uses a crank of radius $R=0.30$ m turning a drum of radius $r=0.05$ m. A load $W=1200$ N hangs from a rope wound on the drum. Find the force $F$ on the crank to lift steadily, and the mechanical advantage.

**(d)** The winch is only $75\%$ efficient (friction in bearings). Find the actual crank force, and explain in one sentence why friction makes you "pay" more force even at constant speed.
